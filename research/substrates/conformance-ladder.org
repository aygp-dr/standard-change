* Status

*Additive research note. =spec.org= governs and nothing here amends it.*
Companion to [[../../adopt/PROFILE.org]], which asks the questions, and to [[../../adopt/generate.sh]],
which acts on the answers. This file answers the question the other two do not:
/given that I cannot have all of it, what should I have, and in what order?/

Epistemic labels are =spec.org='s: [E] established on a named cell, [P]
provisional, [H] hypothesis, [I] inferred. *Everything in this file about a
platform we have not run on is [H].* The rungs themselves are [I] -- they are a
partial order derived from the guards' stated dependencies, not an order anyone
has climbed.

Where this file cites a published standard it also cites the tier from
=research/history/release-standards.org=, because "standard" covers six different kinds of
object and they differ in exactly one respect that matters: *is there a
conformance test, and who can change the rules?*

* The two honest headlines

*First: the minimum viable set is two controls, and neither of them is a guard.*
A gate that can be shown to fail, and a verdict that names the build it is about.
Everything above rung 2 is refinement. A team that adopts only those two has
gained more than a team that adopts six guards on top of gates nobody has
watched reject anything, because the second team's green tells them nothing and
they do not know it.

*Second: the rung most teams cannot reach is rung 6, and it is not their fault.*
Rung 6 requires a production the pipeline can observe, reporting the identity of
the build it is serving. Most deployment platforms will tell you a deploy
*succeeded* and will not tell you what is *running* -- the difference between
intent and evidence, and the difference on which guard 5 and guard 6 both stand.
=spec.org= is explicit that the Deployments API of the forge this repo runs on
does not clear that bar: a deployment is created by a caller and its status is
posted by that same caller, so it records intent with good provenance about /who
claimed it/, not evidence about /what is running/ [E, 2026-09-13].

*The two side rungs hang off rung 1 and are prerequisites for nothing.* That is
the structural shape of the diagram. Serialization protects a *shared* path
from contention, and governance buys *auditability*. Neither makes any change
more correct, and a team that climbs S and G while sitting at rung 1 has built a
process around gates whose verdicts nobody has tested.

** Rung 0 -- No verdict

*What it is:* you have gates, and no gate has ever been watched rejecting
anything.

*What it buys:* nothing, and it is worse than nothing, because the gates are
green and the greenness is structural in everyone's head.

Three tests in this repo passed while asserting nothing: a =typeof === 'string'=
check satisfied by a request-derived value, an escaping assertion satisfied by an
unrelated =&amp;= elsewhere on the page, and an =esc()= call whose deletion failed
zero tests. *All three were found by mutation, never by reading* [E].
=generate.sh= emits nothing at all for a profile at rung 0, which is the only
honest response.

** Rung 1 -- Verified

*Adds:* the gate self-test, guard 2, the evidence record, the fact-ownership
declaration.

*Requires:* a failing fixture per gate. Nothing else. *No forge, no estate, no
second environment.*

*What it buys:* a verdict you can rely on, bound to the content it is about. Two
rules, and both have been paid for here:

- *A suite that cannot be shown to reject its negative fixture returns no
  verdict that run -- not a pass.* This is the one rule that has to be in the
  interface rather than in a convention, because it changes the return type: the
  suite's answer is three-valued.
- *Every observation, approval and deployment record carries the commit it was
  measured against*, and every guard compares that to the head at the moment it
  relies on it. "Staging passed" is not a fact. "Staging passed at =a013270=" is.

*What it costs:* one fixture per gate, and the discipline of writing the fixture
first. In this repo two of the negative fixtures did not fail on the first try.

*Standards footing:* the build-naming half is =in-toto='s =subject= -- tier 2,
open specification with normative language and a self-asserted conformance test.
The self-test half is folklore, tier 6: widely practised, never written down
normatively, and *not standardised by anybody* [E, =research/history/release-standards.org=].

** Rung 2 -- Manifested

*Adds:* the derived manifest, and with it the three-valued =deploys(c)=.

*Requires:* that the set of deployable units a change touches can be computed
from the diff.

*What it buys:* the ability to distinguish /this change deploys nothing/ from /I
could not tell what this change deploys/. That distinction is invisible until it
costs you something, and then it costs you everything: two independent
implementations of guard 6 -- this one, and a clean-room rebuild -- both derived
the deploy set and then treated an *empty* set as *exempt*, and one of them ran
*484 times without ever triggering* [E].

*What it costs:* a glob table you have to maintain, and the rule that a human
edit to the manifest must not survive. A manifest that can be asserted is not a
manifest, it is a wish, and the build ships whatever someone typed.

** Rung 3 -- Authorized

*Adds:* guard 4, and its approval rung.

*Requires:* an environment upstream of production whose observation is what lets
production happen, and -- for the approval rung -- an approver identity the
platform can prove is not the author.

*What it buys:* production is downstream of a measurement someone took against
something real. The rule that makes it worth anything: *the in-runner run does
not authorize production.* The authorizing run is the one against the deployed
environment. Collapse those and you have re-tested the artifact in the place it
was built, which tells you about the build and nothing about the deployment.

*What it costs:* an environment, and the queue for it if it is scarce.

*One honest note on the approval rung [E]:* in this repo the separation between
author and approver exists *by accident*. The proxy reviewer runs as the
author's own token and the forge refuses to let an author approve their own pull
request -- "that refusal is currently the only thing keeping author and reviewer
apart, and it is doing it by accident." If your platform does not refuse the
call, your approval rung is a convention wearing a control's name, and
=generate.sh= emits it saying exactly that on every run.

** Rung 4 -- Current

*Adds:* guard 0 and guard 4b.

*Requires:* that you deploy a *branch head*, not the trunk after merge. Most
teams do not, and lose this rung without ever being asked the question.

*What it buys:* what you verified is still what you ship. Guard 4b is the
expensive one and the one worth understanding, because its content is not the
comparison but *when* the comparison happens: a guard whose subject can change
after it is checked must be re-checked at the moment it is relied on. Guard 0 at
claim time is a convenience that gives fast feedback. Guard 4b at reliance is the
guard.

*It also needs the divergence to be classified rather than collapsed.* Guard 4b
as first written withdrew the staging pass on *any* movement of the trunk, so a
docs merge cost every open branch a full rebase and re-verify and the berth went
back to the pool. Four classes, and they block at two different thresholds:

| class    | what moved                 | guard 0 | guard 4b |
|----------+----------------------------+---------+----------|
| inert    | docs, notes                | pass    | pass     |
| pipeline | gates, workflows           | *block* | pass     |
| artifact | the deployables            | *block* | *block*  |
| hotfix   | a deployable, urgently     | *block* | *block*  |

The asymmetry is the point: =pipeline= blocks guard 0 because your branch would
be verified by the *old* gates, and does not block guard 4b because your tree
would not revert anything.

*Standards footing:* capabilities C4 (an event when the trunk moves) and C5
(verdicts revocable after issue), both marked *rare* by =spec.org=. C5 is
standardised in one adjacent place -- =OSV= is revocable verdicts specified, for a
world in which rollback does not exist. Tier 2.

** Side rung S -- Serialized

*Adds:* guard 1, an exclusive lease with an owner and a deadline, and a reaper.

*Requires:* that the path to production is actually *shared*. If it is not, this
rung is refused and the refusal is the right answer.

*What it buys:* two changes cannot both believe they are the last one in.

*What it costs, and know this before you climb it:* the berth is a throughput
ceiling and it is computable. At 100 changes through one berth with 30-minute
holds: 2.2 days of serialized wall-clock, a 26-hour mean wait, and *the emergency
share reaches 100%* [E, =gates/simulate-gates.py --soak=]. That last number is
not a defect the soak finds -- it is the design meeting its limit. By the time the
queue is that deep, every change has a rational reason to declare itself an
emergency, so a high-volume run through a singleton berth *does not test the
pipeline, it tests the bypass.*

*The remedy is not more berths.* It is a path to production per team, and hard
contracts at the boundary so that "do these compose?" is answerable statically
rather than by serializing. =spec.org= §What this model gets wrong, on purpose is
unambiguous about this: the queue "is not a design, it is what you are left with
when nothing else can tell you whether two changes compose." A reader who adopts
the berth without reading that section has taken home the wrong artifact.

** Side rung G -- Governed

*Adds:* the freeze, the emergency declaration, change windows, the
post-implementation review.

*Requires:* a place to record an estate-level fact that *is not a change*, and a
declarer identity for each.

*What it buys:* auditability, a vocabulary, and -- uniquely -- a place to record a
*forfeit*. It does not buy correctness. No guard is more sound because a window
was booked.

*The single most transferable lesson on this rung, and it is a refusal:* a
freeze recorded on a change is not a freeze. The write that closes the estate to
everyone is the write that exempts its own carrier, so *the one change that must
not proceed becomes the only one that may* [E, PR #48, confirmed by TLC]. Record
it on something that cannot be deployed.

*Standards footing, stated carefully:*

- *ITIL 4 -- tier 4, framework, paywalled.* Its release/deployment vocabulary is
  more useful than its reputation suggests, and it is the only body of practice
  with an object a forfeited window could be an instance of. The purpose
  statements are consistent across several independent restatements, *which is
  corroboration and not verification* -- [E, secondary]. The primary practice
  guides were not read.
- *ISO/IEC 20000-1:2018 §8.5.3 -- tier 1, formal standard, certifiable,
  paywalled.* Its clause title is "Release and deployment management" [E,
  secondary -- ISO's own browsing platform returned 403 and no clause text was
  read]. *[H] on any claim about what the clause actually requires.* It is a real
  standard that does not cover our case, which is worth recording precisely
  because it is the one thing here that is certifiable.
- *DORA -- tier 5, research programme.* It is not a standard and should stop
  being quoted as one. Across nine reports, =change window= / =deployment
  window= / =maintenance window= / =release window= return *zero* hits, and the
  only adjacent finding is that code-freeze periods correlate with *lower*
  performance, in 2016 and 2017 only [E]. So no standard motivates the freeze.
  The research is evidence against it and the framework is merely the only place
  to write it down.
- *The rest of this rung is tier 6 -- folklore.* Change windows as practised,
  "one live version", "rollback means redeploy the previous one". Widely done,
  never written down normatively, and nobody's conformance test.

*The inescapable conclusion from the standards survey, worth quoting because it
sets the expectations for this whole directory:* everything up to and including
publication is standardised, signed and settled. Everything from "it is now
running in an environment" onward is either unstandardised or standardised
without evidence. *Our guards live entirely in the second half.*

** Rung 6 -- Observed

*Adds:* guard 5.

*Requires:* a production the pipeline can reach, serving a build that reports its
own identity.

*What it buys:* you learn what is *running* rather than what you told something
to run. This is the rung that converts the whole pipeline from a story about
intent into a claim about the world, and it is the one most teams cannot reach.

*Two things it must assert, and neither is obvious:*

- *Convergence, not liveness.* N samples per route, one request each,
  cache-busted, and every one must report the expected build. One sample cannot
  distinguish "converged" from "I happened to reach a new replica" -- a fleet 10%
  migrated passes a single sample 10% of the time, which is not a bug you find by
  re-running.
- *One request, both facts.* Reading the status code and the build identity in
  two separate requests lets the first hit an old replica and the second a new
  one, and the pair passes while the estate is mixed.

*What it cannot survive:* a cache it does not bust. This repo's static target
serves =cache-control: max-age=600= [E, =age: 284= observed on a live response],
so "deployed" and "served" can be ten minutes apart, and a convergence check that
trusts a CDN is measuring the CDN.

** Rung 7 -- Ordered

*Adds:* guard 6, production-first.

*Requires:* rung 6, rung 2, and rung 4 -- an observable production, a derivable
deploy set, and branch-head deploys. Three preconditions, and =generate.sh=
refuses this rung when any one of them is missing.

*What it buys:* the trunk never asserts a change that no replica is serving. In
this model the merge is *settlement, not authorization*: production deploys from
the branch, converges, and the trunk follows. Trunk trailing production for the
length of a window is the intended state.

*Why it is stated as a rung rather than assumed:* it was missing from the model,
implemented twice from the same unstated assumption, and *wrong in both* by the
same mechanism [E]. That is the argument for writing predicates down: both
implementations invented the guard and both invented it the same broken way,
because there was nothing to check the invention against.

*And it is the one rung whose conformance requirement includes its own test.*
=spec.org=: "guard 6 cannot be tested without a real estate. A pipeline that can
only test it against an in-process double has not tested it." A conforming
implementation must have a test that fails when the guard is removed, *for all
three values of =deploys(c)=* -- the clean-room rebuild's self-test had exactly the
right two cases and the guard exempted its way past both.

Four rows deserve their reasons written out.

** Guard 5 on a static site is unimplementable as written [E]

GitHub Pages cannot set custom response headers. A GitHub staff member, 2023:
"We don't support this feature today so a =meta= tag unfortunately is the only
way", and re-asked in 2024: "This is not an area that is being prioritized at
the moment." Confirmed by a live =curl -sSI= on 2026-09-13.

*This is a documented absence, which is the one thing a vendor document is
authoritative about*, corroborated empirically. So it is [E], and it means guard
5 as specified cannot run there.

The repair -- publish the build identity as *content*, at =/version.json= -- works
and is weaker in one specific way that must be said: *a header is emitted by the
serving layer per response, and a file is content, and content is cacheable.*
You are then measuring what the CDN chose to hand you.

** Guard 5 is false by construction during a rolling deploy

A fleet mid-roll is *meant* to be serving two builds. Guard 5 as written reports
=UNCONVERGED=, which is correct and is not a failure -- so on a rolling platform
the verdict needs a third state that means "still rolling, ask again", and the
window in which the third state is acceptable needs a bound, or it is an
unbounded excuse. =spec.org= marks this open and provisional [P]. *Do not adopt
guard 5 onto a gradual-rollout platform without deciding what UNCONVERGED means
there first.*

** The tier model is vacuous nearly everywhere, and that is fine

Six of seven columns say *vacuous*. Addresses are only scarce where a namespace
is shared, and the normal case -- a host, an IP, a container, a DNS name per
environment -- does not share one. This is the most estate-specific control in the
repository and the honest thing to say is that it does not travel. What *does*
travel is the invariant underneath it: *a reader should be able to tell what kind
of environment they are looking at, and whether it may promote, from its address
alone, without consulting a policy file.* Encode that however your addresses
work.

** Guard 1 is vacuous on a single-service deployment, and refusing it is the win

If nothing contends for the path, the berth serializes changes that were never in
each other's way. =spec.org='s test: name what it protects, name who pays, and if
the answer is "the queue pays and production gains nothing", it is ceremony.

Two rows are worth expanding.

** "Without a live estate" is the expensive column, and it has only two entries

Everything except guard 4, guard 5, guard 6 and the PIR survives with no
deployable environment at all. That is the good news and it is larger than it
looks: *rungs 1, 2, 4 and both side rungs are attainable by a team that ships a
library and never deploys anything.*

What does not survive is exactly the part that makes claims about the world.
There is no offline version of "production is serving this build", and a pipeline
that fakes one with an in-process double has not tested anything -- it has written
a test whose subject is the test.

** The PIR without an estate is intent, and must say so

A post-implementation review that reports gate durations and a deployment record
is a narrative about what was attempted. It becomes evidence only when a
convergence observation is in it. Both are worth writing. Conflating them is
=spec.org= defect class 3 -- *observation is not intent* -- and the fix is one line:
the record says by name where each value came from. "Someone said it was fine" is
not evidence. "=gates/smoke.sh= observed pass on =a013270=" is.

* Where to stop

A recommendation, and it is [I].

- *Everyone stops at rung 2 or above.* Below it, nothing you have is worth
  reading.
- *Most teams should stop at rung 4*, and spend what they would have spent on
  rungs 6 and 7 on making the deploy set smaller and the path per-team instead.
- *Climb side rung S only if you measured contention.* Not if you anticipated it.
- *Climb side rung G when someone outside the team needs to read what happened*,
  which is a real requirement and not a lesser one. Just do not expect it to make
  a deployment safer.
- *Rung 6 is worth more than everything above rung 2 combined*, if you can reach
  it, because it is the only rung that tells you the truth rather than your own
  intentions. If your platform can expose the build identity of what it is
  serving, do that before anything else on this page.

* Refutation conditions

1. *A team at rung 1 reports that a guard from a higher rung caught something
   rungs 1 and 2 would not have.* Would refute §The two honest headlines.
2. *Guard 5 is implemented on a static target without a custom header and
   without inheriting the cache's answer.* Would refute §Guard 5 on a static site.
3. *A gradual-rollout platform is shown to support guard 5 unmodified.* Would
   refute the =false by construction= verdict and would upgrade a [P] to a
   refuted claim, which is the better outcome.
4. *The tier model turns out to be structural where addresses are not shared.*
   Would refute §The tier model is vacuous nearly everywhere.
5. *A conforming guard 6 is demonstrated against something that is not a real
   estate.* Would refute rung 7's conformance requirement and =spec.org= §Guard 6
   with it -- and it is the single most valuable refutation available here,
   because it would make the most expensive rung cheap.
6. *ISO/IEC 20000-1 §8.5.3 turns out to require something this ladder omits.*
   Would refute the [E, secondary] claim that it is silent on every question
   here. The only way to settle it is to read the clause, which costs money.
