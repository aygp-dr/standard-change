#+TITLE: Rings, channels, and flags: a different shape than blue-green or a three-stage canary
#+DATE: 2026-09-25
#+PROPERTY: header-args :mkdirp t

* Why this note exists

=release-strategies.org= already dates Facebook's three-stage H1/H2/H3
canary (2012-2013) and Fowler's 2010 blue-green/canary naming. Three
patterns are missing that are not variations on those two -- they are a
different *shape* (continuous, subscribable tiers rather than a temporary
rollout percentage), a different *axis* (deploy vs. release, not staging
vs. production), and a different *mechanism* (traffic mirroring, not
traffic splitting).

* Ring/channel deployment: N continuous tiers, not a temporary rollout

A binary blue-green swap and a three-stage canary both converge to 100%
and stop being three stages. A channel model does not converge -- Canary
stays Canary indefinitely, and a user or machine explicitly opts into a
tier rather than being temporarily included in one.

*Chrome.* Beta and Dev channels were both live within weeks of Chrome's
public launch, per the [[https://chromereleases.googleblog.com/2008/09/][Chrome Releases blog, September 2008]]. Canary came
later: Google's [[https://www.chromium.org/getting-involved/chrome-release-channels/][July 22, 2010 announcement]] shortened the stable cadence
to six weeks and introduced Canary as a distinct, more experimental tier.
Canary really is the nightly build: "Canary builds contain changes as soon
as we make them, and are released twice daily with only automated
testing," installable side by side with any other channel
([[https://developer.chrome.com/docs/web-platform/chrome-release-channels][Chrome for Developers: release channels]]).

*Windows Insider.* Launched October 1, 2014 with the first Windows 10
Technical Preview, using the literal word "ring" from day one -- Insider
Fast and Insider Slow, with a Release Preview ring added February 2016.
Microsoft replaced "rings" with "channels" (Dev/Beta/Release Preview) on
June 15, 2020 ([[https://blogs.windows.com/windows-insider/2020/06/15/introducing-windows-insider-channels/][Windows Insider Blog]]); a public Canary channel followed
March 6, 2023. Microsoft is the more literal of the two about "ring" as
the model's own name.

*Debian, much older than either.* Founded August 1993; Debian 1.1
"Buzz," the first *stable* release, shipped June 17, 1996 -- implying an
"unstable" counterpart track already existed by then. The formal
three-way stable/testing/unstable ("sid") structure came later: "testing"
was introduced as its own distribution in **December 2000**, per Debian's
own [[https://www.debian.org/doc/manuals/project-history/detailed.en.html][detailed project history]]: "a new distribution /testing/ was
introduced... packages from unstable that are said to be stable moved to
testing."

*Is "predates by years" an honest claim?* Partially, and precision
matters here. Package-repo tracks and app-release channels solve related
but not identical problems: a package repo gates what enters a shared
archive many other packages transitively depend on (hence "testing" as a
quarantine before promotion), while Chrome/Windows channels gate what one
product ships to one opted-in user. Do not conflate the mechanisms. But
the *structural* claim -- N continuous, subscribable tiers rather than a
temporary rollout -- holds across all three, and Debian's 1996/2000 dates
are eight to eighteen years ahead of Chrome/Windows Insider's 2008/2014.

* Feature flags: the formal articulation of "deploy != release"

*Origin, corrected.* The canonical long-form treatment,
[[https://martinfowler.com/articles/feature-toggles.html][martinfowler.com/articles/feature-toggles.html]], is written by **Pete
Hodgson**, published September 19, 2016 (updated October 9, 2017) -- hosted
on Fowler's site, not authored solely by Fowler. It names four toggle
categories (Release, Experiment, Ops, Permissioning) and states the core
claim directly: toggles "allow incomplete and un-tested codepaths to be
shipped to production as latent code which may never be turned on" -- the
explicit articulation of deploy ≠ release. A shorter bliki entry,
[[https://martinfowler.com/bliki/FeatureToggle.html][FeatureToggle]], traces to around October 2010, earlier than Hodgson's full
treatment.

*Earlier still: Flickr, 2009.* John Allspaw and Paul Hammond, "10+ Deploys
Per Day: Dev and Ops Cooperation at Flickr," Velocity 2009 (June 2009),
names "feature flags" explicitly as one of the practices that let Flickr
decouple deploy from release -- one year before Fowler's bliki entry and
five years before LaunchDarkly's founding. Primary materials:
[[https://www.slideshare.net/jallspaw/10-deploys-per-day-dev-and-ops-cooperation-at-flickr][SlideShare deck]], [[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LdOe18KhtT4][video]], contemporaneous writeup at
[[http://dev2ops.org/2009/06/10-deploys-per-day-dev-and-ops-cooperation-at-flickr/][dev2ops.org, June 2009]].

*LaunchDarkly (founded 2014, Oakland, Edith Harbaugh and John Kodumal)*
does not claim to have invented flags -- their own framing is
productizing/service-ifying a practice that already existed as homegrown
tooling at companies including Facebook and Flickr
([[https://launchdarkly.com/about-us/][about-us]]).

* Shadow traffic / dark launch -- real, but the exact term is weakly sourced

Traces to Facebook Chat, 2008. Engineer Eugene Letuchy described the
technique in Chat-architecture talks (Erlang Factory / CUFP, 2008-2009):
before any UI existed, Facebook pages silently connected to chat servers,
queried presence, and simulated message sends, "without a single UI
element drawn on the page," purely to load-test under real traffic --
[[http://www.erlang-factory.com/upload/presentations/31/EugeneLetuchy-ErlangatFacebook.pdf][Erlang at Facebook: Chat Architecture (PDF)]], corroborated by
[[https://www.infoq.com/news/2008/05/facebookchatarchitecture/][InfoQ's contemporaneous summary]].

*Honest gap.* Facebook's own [[https://engineering.fb.com/2008/05/13/web/facebook-chat/][engineering blog post on Chat]] (May 13, 2008)
documents the rollout but could not be fetched directly in this pass to
confirm it uses the literal phrase "dark launch." Every secondary
retrospective found repeats the Facebook/Letuchy attribution, but none
supplies a dated primary quote using that exact term. The *mechanism* --
mirroring real traffic to unreleased infrastructure without serving its
output -- is solidly attested to 2008. The *name* "dark launch" attached
to it is not independently confirmed from a primary 2008 source in this
pass. Cite the mechanism as [E]; cite the specific term's 2008 origin as
[H].

* What this changes

Three genuinely distinct shapes now sit alongside blue-green and
Facebook's three-stage canary: a continuous N-tier channel (opt-in,
non-converging), a flag (decouples deploy from release entirely, no
traffic-routing mechanism required at all), and shadow traffic (observes a
version under real load with zero user-facing risk, before it is ever a
release candidate). None of the three collapse into either blue-green or
canary -- they answer adjacent but different questions about what "safe to
expose" means.
