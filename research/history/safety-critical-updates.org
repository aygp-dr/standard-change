#+TITLE: When the gate is a certification body: avionics, automotive, and ATMs
#+DATE: 2026-09-26
#+PROPERTY: header-args :mkdirp t

* Why this note exists

Everything else in this corpus's history gates a deploy with a script, a
label, or a platform's own attestation. This note looks at three domains
where the gate is, in whole or part, a regulatory certification body --
arguably the most rigorous gate this survey has looked at anywhere, and
also, honestly, the one with the least documented rollout mechanics once
certification is granted. No forced mapping onto this corpus's own
gate/verdict vocabulary is attempted here: these are certification-body
approvals and fleet-ops processes, not CI pipelines with pass/fail
artifacts, and pretending otherwise would overstate the parallel.

* Avionics: the deepest certification framework, the thinnest rollout story

**The gate.** DO-178C (RTCA, 2011) is the FAA/EASA-recognized means of
compliance (FAA AC 20-115D names it "an acceptable means, but not the only
means"). Five Design Assurance Levels scale directly with failure
severity: A Catastrophic (71 objectives, 30 requiring independent
verification) down to E No effect (0 objectives)
([[https://quality.arc42.org/standards/do-178c][arc42 overview]]; [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DO-178C][Wikipedia]]). Structural coverage escalates with level --
D requires none, C requires statement coverage, B adds decision coverage,
A adds Modified Condition/Decision Coverage (MC/DC) plus resolution down
to compiler-generated object code
([[https://ldra.com/ldra-blog/do-178c-structural-coverage-analysis/][LDRA]]; [[https://www.rapitasystems.com/blog/does-do-178c-require-object-code-structural-coverage][Rapita Systems]]). Full bidirectional requirements traceability is
mandated throughout, most stringent at level A
([[https://www.jamasoftware.com/requirements-management-guide/aerospace-and-defense/do-178c/][Jama Software]]).

**The mechanism.** Historically and still commonly: Loadable Software
Airplane Parts (LSAP), a technician-operated data loader per ARINC
615/615A, on removable media, signed per ARINC 645-1
([[https://www.aviationtoday.com/2010/11/01/field-loadable-software/][Avionics International]]; [[https://www.rtx.com/collinsaerospace/what-we-do/service-and-support/support/loadable-aircraft-part-signing][Collins Aerospace]]). Newer aircraft (787, A350)
run **ARINC 615A-4** over Ethernet, and the 787's "e-enabled" Common Core
System (ARINC 664/AFDX) lets ground systems push updates over an onboard
network at the gate ([[https://www.militaryaerospace.com/commercial-aerospace/article/14224608/next-generation-rockwell-collins-avionics-and-pilot-control-systems-make-first-flight-on-787][Military Aerospace]]). This is not phone-style OTA --
still a certified part number, a gate session, signature verification, no
evidence anywhere of silent in-flight updates.

**Rollout: not found.** No source describes staged or canary rollout for
certified avionics software across a fleet; deployment reads as manually
driven, aircraft by aircraft. Table A-5 and AC 20-115D's own text could
not be fetched directly (paywalled/403); the level/coverage figures above
are corroborated across two independent secondary technical sources, not
confirmed against RTCA's own text -- flagged honestly rather than cited as
primary.

* Automotive: the clearest regulation, and a real incident history

**The gate.** UN Regulation No. 156 entered into force 22 January 2021,
binding for new vehicle *types* from roughly July 2022, extending to all
newly produced vehicles by July 2024
([[https://unece.org/transport/documents/2021/03/standards/un-regulation-no-156-software-update-and-software-update][UNECE]]; dates corroborated via [[https://www.ul.com/sis/insights/software-update-management-systems-according-unece-r156][UL Solutions]] and [[https://autocrypt.io/unr156-securing-vehicle-software-updates/][AUTOCRYPT]] since
UNECE's own PDF returned 403 in this research pass). It requires a
certified organizational **Software Update Management System (SUMS)**,
per-vehicle traceability via **RXSWIN** identifiers readable over OBD
([[https://www.itemis.com/en/glossary/unece-r156/][itemis]]; [[https://diadrom.com/insights/un-r156-sums-requirements][Diadrom]]), and a documented safety/security impact assessment before
any update deploys.

**Relation to ISO 26262.** 26262 is the general electrical/electronic
functional-safety lifecycle standard; R156 governs the update *process*
specifically. A documented gap: 26262 has no normative re-certification
process for post-deployment OTA changes to safety-relevant behavior --
R156's SUMS only partially closes it
([[https://saemobilus.sae.org/papers/analysis-impact-sums-r156-update-functional-safety-2026-26-0618][SAE Mobilus 2026-26-0618]]).

**Tesla, as concrete precedent rather than pop-culture gesture.** 2013:
an OTA update raised Model S ride height after underbody fire incidents
([[https://www.greencarreports.com/news/1092515_tesla-model-s-electric-car-software-updates-since-2012][GreenCarReports]]). January 2014: NHTSA recall 14V006000 (29,222 vehicles,
charger-adapter fire risk) was resolved purely via OTA throttling --
widely cited as the first OTA-resolved automotive recall
([[https://techcrunch.com/2014/01/14/tesla-issues-recall-on-about-29222-model-s-power-adapters-that-could-cause-fires/][TechCrunch]]; [[https://www.technologyreview.com/2014/01/14/174576/tesla-motors-over-the-air-repairs-are-the-way-forward/][MIT Technology Review]]).

**Rollout.** An industry pattern exists but is not R156-mandated: push to
a canary cohort, monitor telemetry and fault codes, expand, halt or
roll back on failure ([[https://www.redstoneota.com/ota-staged-rollout-safe-update-campaigns/][RedstoneOTA]]). No OEM-published rollout percentage
was found -- this is pattern-level industry practice, not a verified,
per-vendor documented figure.

* ATMs: genuinely thin, and that thinness is itself the finding

**The gate.** PCI PTS (PIN Transaction Security) is the one substantive
thread: a firmware change to an approved device requires re-assessment by
a PCI-approved lab, delta-evaluated unless the OS changes wholesale (PCI
SSC materials, via search-indexed snippet -- direct PDF text unverified,
flagged [H]). PTS POI v6 adds a hard three-year firmware re-approval
expiration, a real change from purely vulnerability-driven re-evaluation
in prior versions ([[https://atmavailability.com/navigating-the-transition-a-definitive-analysis-of-pci-pts-poi-v5-and-the-strategic-imperative-of-v6/][ATM Availability]]; program mechanics from
[[https://listings.pcisecuritystandards.org/documents/PTS_Program_Guide_v2.0_Final.pdf][PCI SSC's own Program Guide]]). Whether an ATM software update specifically
triggers EMVCo re-certification could not be confirmed publicly -- EMVCo's
own FAQ returned only terms-of-use text.

**The mechanism.** Marketing-level only from both dominant vendors -- no
technical spec found. NCR Atleos/APTRA describes centralized remote
system/config management across mixed fleets
([[https://www.ncratleos.com/banking/atm-itm/software/management][NCR Atleos]]); Diebold Nixdorf's Vynamic View describes automated
distribution, scheduled updates, fleet monitoring
([[https://www.dieboldnixdorf.com/en-us/banking/portfolio/software/availability-management/][Diebold Nixdorf]]). A third vendor, Hyosung, is more explicit and useful:
they describe moving from USB/floppy media to HTTPS-based centralized push
with scheduling ([[https://hyosungamericas.com/bulletins/remote-updates/][Hyosung]]) -- implying physical media was recent industry
practice, not fully retired even now for older or offline units.

**Rollout: not found.** Staged or percentage-based rollout for ATM fleets
specifically does not appear in any of the three vendors' own public
material. Canary/ring deployment is well-documented for device fleets in
general elsewhere in this corpus -- its absence here, specifically for
ATMs, from vendors who otherwise publish detailed fleet-management
marketing, is itself worth recording as a real gap in the public record,
not a search failure.

* What this comparison actually shows

Avionics has the deepest, most rigorous certification framework in this
entire corpus and almost nothing documented about how an approved update
actually reaches a fleet at scale. Automotive has the newest, most
specific regulation (R156) plus a real, dated, citable incident history
(Tesla's 2014 recall). ATMs have one real regulatory thread surrounded by
vendor silence. None of the three converge on a shared rollout vocabulary
the way software deployment has converged on canary/blue-green -- worth
noting as a real difference in kind: certification gates *whether* an
update may exist at all; it says comparatively little, across all three
domains, about *how* it reaches the fleet once it does.
