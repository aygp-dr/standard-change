#+TITLE: Where "gate" actually comes from -- two lineages, not one, neither of them software
#+DATE: 2026-09-26
#+PROPERTY: header-args :mkdirp t

* Why this note exists

This repo's own core vocabulary is literally "gates" (the =gates/=
directory, =gate.yml=, spec.org's own line "a gate that can be shown to
fail") and numbered "guards." =research/findings/compliance-naming.org=
already covers Six Sigma as a compliance-level naming convention, but this
corpus had never asked where "gate," as a go/no-go quality checkpoint,
actually comes from. It predates software CI/CD entirely, and -- the real
finding here -- it comes from *two separate lineages* that happen to share
a word, not one continuous history.

* Lineage one: Shewhart, and the idea a gate is meaningful only if it can fail

Walter Shewhart, at Western Electric's Hawthorne Works / Bell Telephone
Laboratories, wrote a roughly one-page internal memo on **May 16, 1924**
containing what his boss George Edwards later called "a simple diagram
which we would all recognize today as a schematic control chart" -- widely
treated as the first control chart ([[https://www.qualitydigest.com/inside/quality-insider-column/shewhart-s-great-discovery.html][Quality Digest, "Shewhart's Great
Discovery"]]; [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_chart][Wikipedia: Control chart]]; [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_A._Shewhart][Wikipedia: Walter A. Shewhart]]). His book,
*Economic Control of Quality of Manufactured Product* (D. Van Nostrand
Company, New York, 1931; reissued by ASQ Quality Press as a 50th-
anniversary edition in 1981, ISBN 9780873890205), formalizes the
distinction this corpus's own phrase directly echoes: **common-cause
variation** is expected and should not trigger action; **special-cause
variation** is a real signal that warrants stopping and investigating. A
control chart that never flags anything is not evidence of a stable
process -- it's evidence the chart isn't watching the right thing. That is
the same argument as this repo's own "a gate that can be shown to fail" --
=gate-selftest='s whole reason to exist -- stated a century earlier, in a
different discipline, for a different reason.

*The Shewhart/Deming cycle attribution, resolved.* Deming took these ideas
to postwar Japan via JUSE (the Union of Japanese Scientists and Engineers)
starting in **1950**. Popular usage calls the resulting improvement loop
the "Deming cycle" or PDCA (Plan-Do-Check-Act); Deming himself preferred
"Shewhart cycle" and credited Shewhart directly -- confirmed by the Deming
Institute's own historical material ([[https://deming.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/PDSA-history-Ron-Moen.pdf][Ronald Moen, "Foundation and History
of the PDSA Cycle," deming.org]]) and its "Circling Back" piece. Shewhart's
own 1939 version was a three-part specify-produce-inspect cycle; Deming's
1950 lectures modified it into a four-part design-produce-market-
test/redesign form. This is a real, documented misattribution -- the same
shape as the GitHub-flow merge-order correction already elsewhere in this
corpus's history (=pipeline-history.org=) -- worth naming precisely rather
than defaulting to the popular "Deming cycle" label.

*ASQ's own glossary sidesteps the attribution question entirely --
confirmed directly, not inferred.* ASQ's Quality Glossary, letter P
([[https://asq.org/quality-resources/quality-glossary/p][asq.org/quality-resources/quality-glossary/p]], read directly), defines
the entry plainly as "Plan-do-check-act (PDCA) cycle" -- neither "Deming
cycle" nor "Shewhart cycle" appears anywhere in ASQ's own term or
definition. This is not an oversight born of not knowing the history:
the same glossary page separately and explicitly credits Deming by name
for a different concept -- "Profound knowledge, system of: Defined by
W. Edwards Deming" -- so ASQ is demonstrably willing to attribute a term
to him when the term is his own. Choosing not to for PDCA reads as a
deliberate sidestep of the exact naming dispute this section untangles,
not an absence of awareness.

* Lineage two: Cooper's Stage-Gate -- a different concept, a different word choice, entirely unconnected

**Corrected against the working assumption going in.** There is no clean
1986 paper titled to match. What the record actually shows: Robert G.
Cooper's foundational empirical work is earlier and differently framed --
"The Dimensions of Industrial New Product Success and Failure," *Journal
of Marketing*, 1979, and "The NewProd System: The Industry Experience."
His first book-length statement, *Winning at New Products* (Addison-
Wesley/Basic Books, 1986), was reviewed in the *Journal of Product
Innovation Management* ([[https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1540-5885.340307][Cooper, 1986, JPIM, DOI 10.1111/1540-5885.340307]]).
But the term "Stage-Gate," and the literal go/kill/hold/recycle gate
terminology, first appears in print in **1988**: Cooper, R.G., "The New
Product Process: A Decision Guide for Managers," *Journal of Marketing
Management*, 3(3), 238-255.

*What a Stage-Gate gate actually is.* Not a pass/fail statistical-defect
check -- a forward-looking, go/kill/hold/recycle resource-commitment
decision, evaluating both a project's readiness *and* its ongoing business
value, made by people with authority to kill the project entirely
([[https://www.stage-gate.com/blog/the-stage-gate-model-an-overview/][Stage-Gate International's own retrospective]]). "Stage-Gate" is now a
registered trademark -- Stage-Gate International in the US, Cooper
personally in Canada and Europe. This is a genuinely different concept
from Shewhart's control chart, arriving at the same English word from a
completely separate discipline (new-product-development management, not
statistical process control) a full generation after Shewhart's 1924
memo, with no documented connection between the two lineages found in
this research pass.

* ASQ and ISO 9001: checked directly, and the negative result holds

ASQ (American Society for Quality) maintains an honorary-members page on
Shewhart ([[https://asq.org/about-asq/honorary-members/shewhart][asq.org/about-asq/honorary-members/shewhart]]), confirming his
place in the field's own institutional memory. The earlier pass of this
note left one question unresolved -- ASQ's own glossary pages returned 403
to a plain fetch, so the absence of a "quality gate" entry could not be
told apart from a blocked request. **Resolved directly**, via a real
browser session rather than a script: ASQ's "Quality Tools A to Z" index
([[https://asq.org/quality-resources/quality-tools][asq.org/quality-resources/quality-tools]]) lists every tool from A3 report
through X-Y graph, and neither "gate," "quality gate," nor "stage-gate"
appears anywhere in it. The glossary's own G page
([[https://asq.org/quality-resources/quality-glossary/g][asq.org/quality-resources/quality-glossary/g]]) confirms the same thing
more precisely: there is a "Gatekeeper" entry, but it means something
unrelated -- "a timekeeper; in team meetings, a designated individual who
helps monitor the team's use of allocated time" -- not a checkpoint. What
ASQ's own glossary actually calls the pass/fail-against-specification
concept is a different term entirely: **"Go/no-go: State of a unit or
product. Two parameters are possible: go (conforms to specifications) and
no-go (does not conform to specifications)."** ASQ has the underlying
concept, named plainly, and simply never called it a gate.

The C page ([[https://asq.org/quality-resources/quality-glossary/c][asq.org/quality-resources/quality-glossary/c]]) also confirms the
Shewhart connection precisely at the vocabulary level: "Common causes:
Causes of variation that are inherent in a process over time... Also see
'special causes'" -- ASQ's own glossary still uses Shewhart's exact
distinction, a century on, even without naming him in that specific
entry.

ISO 9001:2015, Clause 8.3.4 ("Design and development controls"), does
describe staged design reviews with defined acceptance criteria at each
stage before proceeding -- commonly described by consultants and auditors
as "stage gates" (preliminary/detail/critical design review is a typical
breakdown). But the standard's own text, as far as this pass could
determine, does not itself use the word "gate" -- that vocabulary appears
to be auditor and consultant gloss on the clause, not ISO's own language.
Cite this distinction carefully: ISO 9001's staged-review framing is real
and load-bearing for its own purpose, but it is a third, distinct
framing from both Shewhart/Deming's statistical gate and Cooper's
business-decision gate, and calling it a "quality gate" is common usage,
not the standard's own term.

* Software's "Quality Gate": convergent naming, not documented lineage

SonarQube is the clearest software example of the literal phrase --
its own documentation defines a Quality Gate functionally: "a set of
conditions against which the code is measured during analysis... is my
project ready for release?" ([[https://docs.sonarsource.com/][docs.sonarsource.com]]). No SonarSource
documentation, blog post, or founder interview found in this pass credits
Shewhart, Deming, or Cooper's Stage-Gate work as an explicit lineage --
the term appears to have been adopted on its own functional merits, not
inherited from either manufacturing tradition. Stated honestly rather than
forced into a tidy narrative: the *structural* resemblance to Cooper's
go/kill decision gates (a checkpoint that blocks progression against
defined criteria) is real and strong, but no primary source ties software's
usage back to either manufacturing lineage explicitly. This is convergent
naming -- two fields solving a similar problem and reaching for the same
word independently -- not evidence of a documented line of descent.

* What this changes for how this repo's own vocabulary should be read

This repo's own "gate" sits closer to Shewhart's lineage than Cooper's,
whether or not that was a deliberate choice: =gate-selftest= exists
specifically because a gate that cannot be shown to fail proves nothing --
Shewhart's common-cause/special-cause argument, not Cooper's
go/kill/hold/recycle business decision. "Guard," this repo's other core
term, maps to neither lineage found here -- worth flagging as a genuinely
open naming question this note does not resolve, not smoothed over. Two
real historical lineages exist for "gate," they are not the same idea,
and neither one is software's invention -- software, including this
repo's own vocabulary, arrived at the word by a third, unconnected route.
