#+TITLE: Metaphors - slipway
#+SUBTITLE: Borrowed domains for reasoning about labels as control and audit
#+OPTIONS: toc:2 num:t ^:nil
#+STARTUP: overview

* What this is

This repo is an argument about guardrails: which system dependencies are
declared, and how labels and pipelines act as a *control* mechanism (they
decide what may happen next) and an *audit* mechanism (they record what did).
Those two jobs pull in opposite directions, and most of the defects found here
come from a label being asked to do both.

Metaphors are load-bearing for that argument, not decoration. A borrowed domain
that has solved the same problem for a century tells you which distinctions are
real. Companion to [[./vocabulary.org][~vocabulary.org~]], which names the terms; this file names the
*pictures* behind them.

Each metaphor below is checked against one question, because that question is
where the control job and the audit job came apart.

* The question these four were built for

On 2026-09-15 the owner ruled: a merged change must not carry ~change:end~.

: it's merged, i know the change, if needed, has ended

The pipeline had been clearing every transient label at settlement and then
writing ~change:end~ as a tombstone. The owner's objection was to the shape of
it:

: the recursive nature of clear all labels then add change end then clear
: change end is weird -- like having a bunch of dishes on the counter, then
: putting all in the dishwasher then putting a postit note indicating that
: you cleaned up the kitchen then tossing the postit note

The resolution is not "drop the tombstone". It is that *the tombstone is only
worth writing where no authoritative record exists*. A merge is recorded by the
forge, in ~mergedAt~, which the pipeline does not own and cannot contradict. An
abort is recorded by nothing.

The general rule, which is what the metaphors are for:

#+begin_quote
Two records of one fact can disagree. The platform's cannot. So a label that
restates a fact the platform already owns is not a second record - it is a
future contradiction with a timestamp.
#+end_quote

slipway has the receipt: PR #52 carried ~change:end~ through an entire
successful deploy while still open. ~mergedAt~ could not have done that.

* Four metaphors

** Harbour: the departure log and the chalk on the berth

The harbour master keeps a departure log. A ship that sails is in it - stamped,
timed, owned by the port, not writable by the crew. That is ~mergedAt~.

Chalking =DEPARTED= on the empty berth is a second record of the same sailing.
It is convenient, it is local, and it is *wrong the moment it disagrees* - a
berth chalked =DEPARTED= with a ship still alongside is precisely #52. Nobody
resolves it by consulting the chalk.

But a ship that never sails - charter abandoned, towed out, scrubbed - leaves no
departure entry at all. Now the chalk is the only thing separating /we stood
this down deliberately/ from /the harbour master cleared the berth because the
fees lapsed/. Same mark, opposite value, decided entirely by whether the port's
own log has an entry.

- control :: the berth is free, so the next ship may enter
- audit :: why it is free, and the log answers that only for ships that sailed

** Air traffic control: the strip that goes in the landed bay

A flight that lands is in the arrival log and its progress strip moves to the
landed bay. The system records it; the controller does not have to.

Writing =LANDED= on the strip and leaving it in the active rack is the failure
mode. The rack is the control surface - what is in it is what you are still
separating - and a strip that says =LANDED= while sitting in the active rack is
a fact fighting the instrument next to it. The radar wins, and the strip has
cost you the seconds you spent reconciling them.

A flight cancelled at the gate never generates an arrival. Then the notation on
the strip is the whole record, and it distinguishes /cancelled/ from /lost from
the sequence/ - which is the difference between a tidy day and an incident
report.

This is also why slipway's queue is AIS-shaped rather than ATC-shaped: labels
broadcast, nobody holds the picture. See =.claude/agents/queue-atc.md=.

- control :: what is in the active rack is what still needs separating
- audit :: the arrival log, for everything that arrived

** Racing: the transponder loop and the pit board

The car crossing the line is timed by the loop. The timing system owns that
fact absolutely; it is the reason the sport can be adjudicated at all.

=FINISHED= chalked on the pit board is a restatement, and the board is the wrong
place for it - the board is for instructions to a driver who is still driving.
A board reading =FINISHED= to a car still circulating is an instruction, not a
record, and it will be obeyed.

A car retired in the garage never crosses the loop. =RET - engine= on the sheet
is then the only record, and it carries the one thing the timing system cannot
infer: that this was a decision rather than a disappearance. Classification
exists for exactly the cars the transponder cannot classify.

- control :: the pit board, read by someone who will act on it
- audit :: the timing loop, for everything that finished; the sheet, for
  everything that did not

** Kitchen: the ticket rail and the void

An order that goes out rings out on the POS. The sale is recorded by the system
that takes the money, which is the one record nobody is allowed to edit.

Spiking the ticket *and* writing =SERVED= on it is the post-it on the clean
dishwasher. Worse, the rail is the control surface - tickets on the rail are
work outstanding - so a ticket marked =SERVED= still hanging on the rail is a
dish the line will cook twice.

An order killed mid-service - allergy, walkout, comp - never rings out. =VOID -
walkout= written before spiking is the only record, and it is the one the
manager reads at close. Without it a killed ticket is indistinguishable from one
that fell behind the pass, and those have very different consequences.

- control :: what hangs on the rail is what still has to be cooked
- audit :: the POS for everything sold, the void note for everything not

* What all four agree on

Every domain independently separates two surfaces, and the separation is always
the same one:

| surface           | question it answers        | who owns it   | slipway         |
|-------------------+----------------------------+---------------+-----------------|
| the working board | what may happen next       | the operator  | the labels      |
| the system record | what happened              | the platform  | ~mergedAt~, the forge |

Three consequences this repo has paid for:

1. *A label on the control surface is an instruction.* The pit board is obeyed.
   The rail is cooked. The rack is separated. A terminal label left on an open
   change is not a stale note, it is a live instruction - which is why
   ~gates/pr-state-audit.py~ treats =terminal-on-open= (I3) as a finding rather
   than as untidiness.

2. *Restating the platform's record creates a contradiction, never a backup.*
   The chalk, the =LANDED= strip, the =FINISHED= board, the =SERVED= ticket.
   All four domains treat this as an error, not as redundancy.

3. *The annotation earns its place exactly where the platform has no entry.*
   The abandoned charter, the gate cancellation, the retirement, the void. In
   slipway: ~change/abort.sh~ keeps ~change:end~, and it is right to.

The modelled form is ~NoTombstoneOnMerged~ in [[../tla/Labels.tla][~tla/Labels.tla~]], guarded by the
~MergeIsTheTombstone~ constant so the invariant can be made to fail.

* Four more, from computing

The four above are physical domains, and they were chosen for the *control vs
audit* split. The berth's other question -- what kind of lock is it, and what
may expire it -- is better served by domains that have a specification rather
than a picture. A borrowed word from computing comes with a man page.

** UNIX: advisory locking, and the atomic create

=flock(2)= and =fcntl= locks are *advisory*: the kernel records the holder and
refuses a conflicting request, and a process that never asks is never stopped.
Mandatory locking is the other kind, and almost nobody uses it.

That is the berth exactly, and it is the caution this file already states in
other words -- "a harbour has physical exclusion; a label does not". UNIX has
the domain's own word for it, so we should use it: *the berth is an advisory
lock*. Guard 1 is the cooperating process asking first. Anything writing
=deploy:staging= without asking is not breaking the lock, it is declining to
participate in one, and that is what five identities did in one day.

The second UNIX word is the one =change/lock.sh= is missing. The idiom for a
correct acquire is not read-then-write; it is =open(O_CREAT|O_EXCL)= or
=mkdir=, both atomic, both failing loudly when someone got there first.
=lock.sh acquire= reads the holder, decides, then writes unconditionally --
which is =test= followed by =set= with a window in between.

- control :: the lock is advisory; it works only because everyone asks
- audit :: the holder and the time, in the lock record

** UNIX: the PID file, and why a stale one is not proof

=/var/run/foo.pid= plus an age check is =change/lock.sh= in one sentence: a
record naming the holder and when it started, declared stale after an interval.

It also carries the classic defect, and we have our own version of it. A PID
file goes wrong when the PID is *reused*: the file looks live, the process it
names is not the one that wrote it. Our analogue is the re-label -- #94 added
=deploy:staging= fourteen times during experiment 022, so "the label is
present" and "this claim is the one that acquired it" are different facts. That
is why =change/berth.sh= ages the *last labeled event not followed by an
unlabeled*, rather than the label's presence.

** Filesystems: the lease, and the renewal that does not exist

NFSv4 states P1's shape precisely and in the domain's own words: a client holds
a *lease* on a delegation, the lease has a term, and the client must *renew* it
or the server reclaims. Linux =fcntl(F_SETLEASE)= is the same idea locally.
=Stale file handle= is the error for a handle that outlived what it named.

The gap this exposes: five-properties P1 says "an unrenewed lease lapses", and
*we have no renew operation*. Nothing a holder does extends the berth, so the
term is measured from acquisition and never refreshed, and a long legitimate
deployment ages exactly like an abandoned one. A lease without renewal is a
timeout wearing a lease's name.

- control :: the delegation is held while the lease is live
- audit :: the server's state, not the client's belief

** Event stacks: the log, the projection, and the fencing token

The queue is *AIS-shaped* -- that finding is above and it is the right one.
Event-stack computing has the same shape with a specification attached.

*Log versus projection.* In event sourcing the log is authoritative and every
view is a projection rebuilt from it. The labels are a projection; =mergedAt=
is the log. "Restating the platform's record creates a contradiction, never a
backup" is the projection-versus-log rule, and it is why a projection may be
rebuilt at will but may never be *written to* as though it were the log.

*Consumer groups.* A Kafka consumer holds a partition under a lease with a
session timeout; exceeding =max.poll.interval.ms= evicts the member and
triggers a rebalance. That is =BERTH_EXPIRY=auto= precisely, and it is the
most-deployed instance of the thing we are building.

*The fencing token, and why manual is the default.* Kleppmann's argument about
distributed locks is that a lease plus timer eviction is *unsafe* unless every
write carries a monotonically increasing token that the resource checks and
refuses when stale. Without one, an evicted holder that resumes -- from a
pause, a long GC, a slow network -- writes after losing the lock and nothing
downstream can tell. Kafka has this: the generation id. We do not:
=targets/*/deploy.sh= carries no token and the estate would accept a write from
an evicted holder.

So =BERTH_EXPIRY=auto= is exactly the configuration Kleppmann describes as
unsafe, and the owner's ruling that expiry is *manual by default* is the
correct one for a system with no fencing. The option exists; the default
declines it, and now the reason has a citation.

- control :: the lease, and the token that makes eviction survivable
- audit :: the log; the projection is never the record

** Schedulers: starvation, aging, and what FIFO would cost

A run queue is where "no FIFO" has a name and a remedy. Experiment 022 is a
textbook *starvation*: #94 lost five claim rounds over twenty-four minutes, and
completion order was #95 #93 #96 #92 #94 -- not arrival order. UNIX schedulers
answer this with *aging*: a runnable process that keeps losing has its priority
raised until it cannot lose again. A mutex with no queue has nowhere to put the
age, which is why the remedy has to be a queue rather than a better lock.

Two more the scheduler literature supplies, and both are arguments against
reaching for FIFO reflexively:

- *The convoy effect.* FIFO plus one long holder makes every short change wait
  behind it. A schedule would give us ordering and this pathology in the same
  move.
- *Priority inversion.* A low-priority holder blocks a high-priority waiter --
  =itil:emergency= arriving while an ordinary change holds the berth. The
  classic remedies are inheritance and the ceiling protocol;
  =research/history/precedence-and-locks.org= reaches the same place from ITIL,
  and already separates *type* from *priority* the way ServiceNow does.

- control :: the run queue and the policy over it
- audit :: accounting, which is a different subject and a different structure

** The deli counter: take a number, and the trouble with taking two

The owner's picture -- a ticket queue where you might take a couple of numbers
because your release could fail -- is not a loose analogy. Both halves are
named, and one of them is a live gap.

*The deli counter is an algorithm.* Lamport's *Bakery Algorithm* (1974, "A New
Solution of Dijkstra's Concurrent Programming Problem") is the bakery ticket
made formal: each process takes a number, and the lowest number goes next.
Lamport chose it because it needs no atomic operation underneath -- fairness
comes from the numbering, not from the hardware. That is precisely the property
a label-based berth would want, since labels have no atomic operation either.

And it gives the rule the picture implies: *each process takes exactly one
number.* The bakery's fairness guarantee is stated over one ticket per
participant. Take two and you have not cheated the implementation, you have
left the algorithm it was proved under.

*Taking two tickets is also named.* Dean and Barroso call it a *hedged request*
or *backup request* ("The Tail at Scale", CACM 56(2), 2013): issue the same
work twice, take whichever completes first, because the tail is worse than the
duplication. That is exactly "my release might fail, so enter me twice", and in
their setting it is a *good* idea -- which is the interesting part. Hedging is
rational for the individual and corrosive for the queue, and the thing that
decides which you get is whether the queue has *admission control*.

*We have none.* =change/schedule.sh block= validates the groups, validates the
duration against a floor, then walks to the back of the queue -- and never asks
whether this change already holds a window. Two calls, two windows, each an
entitlement to the path. The comment at =:120= says the quiet part: "Without
=--now= this never refuses for a clash; it places itself behind whatever is
already booked."

Hedging is also *rational* here, not merely possible, and ADR 0003 is why:
=reap.sh= deliberately does not rebook, so a lapsed window costs you a full
re-request. An author who expects to fail is correct to take two tickets, and
the only thing preventing a queue of hedges is that nobody has tried.

The fix is one line of the domain's own vocabulary -- admission control, one
ticket per identity -- and it belongs in =block=, not in the reaper.

** Ejection, timeout, debounce: three different refusals

The owner named these together and they are three distinct mechanisms, which
is worth keeping apart because each answers a different question.

*Timeout* is the lease's term, and it is already above: when does the holder
stop being the holder. =BERTH_TTL_MIN=.

*Debounce* is about the *requester*, and we need it. In eventing, debounce
collapses a burst into one act; *request coalescing* (nginx =proxy_cache_lock=,
Varnish) is the same idea for identical in-flight work. Experiment 022 measured
the un-debounced case: #94 added =deploy:staging= *fourteen times*, each one a
fresh claim, none of them distinguishable from the others by the label alone.
A debounce on acquisition would have collapsed those into one claim and one
refusal, and =change/berth.sh= has to ignore all but the last labeled event
precisely because no debounce exists upstream of it.

*Ejection* is about the *repeat failer*, and it is the one with no analogue
here at all. Envoy calls it *outlier ejection*: a host that fails often enough
is removed from the pool for a cooldown that grows with each ejection, then
readmitted on probation. Nygard's circuit breaker is the same shape. Applied
here it would say: a change that has claimed the berth and failed N times is
not eligible to claim it again for M minutes -- which is the only mechanism in
this family that protects *other changes* from one change's thrash.

Experiment 022 is the case for it. #94 took five claim rounds over
twenty-four minutes; every one of those rounds was a berth acquisition that
displaced nobody but delayed everybody. Ejection is what a scheduler does with
a process that keeps faulting, and it is the missing third term:

| mechanism | answers | subject | have it? |
|---+---+---+---|
| timeout | when does the holder stop holding | the holder | yes -- =berth.sh= |
| debounce | how many claims is one intent | the requester | no |
| ejection | who may not claim again yet | the repeat failer | no |
| admission control | how many tickets may one change hold | the queue | no |

*And note the ordering.* Ejection and admission control only make sense once a
queue exists, because both are policies *over* a waiting line. Debounce does
not: it is worth having today, against a mutex with no queue at all, and it is
the cheapest of the four.

* On borrowing domains at all

These are reasoning aids and they are not free. Three cautions earned here:

- A metaphor that fits too well stops you looking. slipway's queue looked like
  ATC for weeks; it is AIS - decentralised broadcast with no authority - and
  the ATC picture hid the fact that *nobody was holding the picture*.
- Borrowed domains carry their own failure modes, which may not transfer. A
  harbour has physical exclusion; a label does not. The berth singleton (I1) is
  enforced by an auditor precisely because nothing physical enforces it.
- Prefer the domain's own word once it fits. =berth=, =window=, =freeze= and
  =slot= are all borrowed and all now load-bearing terms in
  [[./vocabulary.org][~vocabulary.org~]]. That is the metaphor having done its job and retired.
