#+SUBTITLE: What these two strategies would mean for this repo's guards, measured where possible
#+DATE: 2026-09-13

* Status

*Research notes. Not spec, not an ADR, not a proposal.* Nothing here is
adopted and nothing here amends =spec.org=. The question is narrow: the repo
has a measured pathological result (ADR-0001: twenty changes on one shared
deployable surface complete *1 of 20* at one berth and *1 of 20* at eight),
and two well-known strategies are routinely offered as the answer to exactly
that shape of problem. This file asks what each would actually buy, against
this repo's own guards and vocabulary, and reports numbers rather than
intuitions.

Epistemic labels are =spec.org='s. Simulation results are [E] — run on hydra
(FreeBSD 14.4-RELEASE-p6 amd64, Python 3.11.15, 2026-09-13) via
=sim/train_sim.py=, a new file; =sim/pipeline_sim.py= and
=sim/test_scenarios.py= are unmodified and still pass, and the baseline below
is driven *through* =pipeline_sim.World= so the comparison is anchored to the
engine Hypothesis has already beaten on.

The conclusion, stated first so nothing below has to be read to get it:

#+begin_quote
Release trains *do* fix the 1/20 contention: the simulator measures *23×* less
revalidation work per shipped change (13.63 → 0.58 validation runs) at a
one-hour cadence, against an eight-berth per-change pipeline. Mainline-only
deployment does *not* — it removes a different class of problem. And *neither*
fixes =itil:emergency=, because merging to main records intent and guard 4b
needs evidence. The throughput-optimal train size is also, on this repo's own
balance curve, a train whose latency routes essentially all traffic onto the
one path that skips staging — so the amortization is real and the optimum is
unreachable.
#+end_quote

* The two strategies, in this repo's vocabulary

** Release trains

Work marked ready collects on a branch. The train departs on a cadence;
whatever is ready rides, whatever is not waits for the next one.

Stated as =spec.org= §Domain model would state it: *a train is a change*. It
has a base (the trunk tip at cut time), a head (the branch tip at departure),
and a manifest that is the union of its cars' manifests. It makes *one* berth
claim for N units of intent. Guard 2 runs once on the branch head; guard 4's
=staged(H(c))= is satisfied by staging the branch; the merge to trunk is a
single merge event, so guard 4b has one subject where it previously had N.

That last sentence is the entire mechanical difference, and it is the whole of
the amortization claim. Drawn as the fan-out that guard 4b actually produces:

#+begin_src mermaid :eval never-export
flowchart TB
  subgraph pc["per-change: every merge invalidates every peer"]
    M1["merge of c1<br/>class = artifact"]
    M1 --> R2["revalidate c2"]
    M1 --> R3["revalidate c3"]
    M1 --> Rn["revalidate c...N"]
  end
  subgraph tr["release train: one merge, one subject"]
    T["merge of the train<br/>class = artifact"]
    T --> RT["revalidate the NEXT train<br/>(one run, N cars)"]
  end
#+end_src

** Mainline-only deployment

Features are independently tested, then merged to main. Every deployment is a
deployment *of main* — never of a branch.

This is a claim about *what the deployable artifact is*, not about when it
ships. It is orthogonal to trains: you can run a train that departs by merging
to main and deploying main, and that combination is the only one in this note
where the two strategies genuinely compose (§Services deployed over time).

** Where a verdict can be bound

The two strategies differ in *which* of three objects carries the verdict that
authorizes a production deployment. C2 — "verdicts bound to a content hash,
not a branch or a change id" — is what makes this the right question.

#+begin_src mermaid :eval never-export
flowchart LR
  A["PR head H(c)<br/><i>guard 2 runs here</i>"]
  B["merge commit main@sha<br/><i>usually NO verdict</i>"]
  C["served artifact<br/><i>guard 5 checks identity,<br/>not safety</i>"]
  A -->|"merge — an ASSERTION,<br/>not a measurement"| B
  B -->|deploy| C
  classDef gap fill:#fff3cd,stroke:#8a6d3b,stroke-width:2px
  class B gap
#+end_src

Per-change today: the verdict is on =A=, =B= is created by the merge and is
never validated as such, =C= is checked only for identity. A release train
moves the verdict onto a tree that *is* =B= for N changes at once.
Mainline-only insists the thing deployed is a descendant of =B=, which is a
statement about =C=, and says nothing about whether =B= has a verdict.

* What each buys and costs, guard by guard

| Guard | Predicate | Per-change (today) | Release train | Mainline-only |

| *2* | =∀g ∈ suite : g(H(c)) = pass= | N suite runs, re-run on every invalidation | one authorizing run on the branch tip. | *4* | =staged(H(c)) ∨ emergency= | staging per change; staging is the scarce singleton | staging per *train*, and the batch staged is exactly the tree that ships — a strictly stronger statement than today's | staging of =main@sha=; same cost, better-named subject |

Two entries are the ones to argue with, and both are below: guard 4b under
mainline-only, and guard 5 under rolling deploys.

* Mainline-only does not touch guard 4b

The tempting reading is that deploying main makes divergence moot: if everyone
deploys main, nothing can be behind main. That is false, and the reason is
worth spelling out because it is the same reason the repo evaluates guard 4b
"at reliance, not at request".

Ask precisely what is validated, and when. There are three arrangements and
none of them closes the gap.

1. *Validate the PR pre-merge, then merge and deploy.* The tree with a verdict
   is =PR head ⊕ main@t=. The tree that deploys is =main@t+1=, produced by the
   merge. Those are different trees whenever anything else merged in between,
   which under a shared artifact surface is the common case, not the rare one.
   This is ADR-0001's 1/20 mechanism verbatim: each change was green against a
   main that has since moved. Mainline-only changes the *name* of what deploys
   and not one thing about the divergence.

2. *Validate main post-merge, deploy the last green sha.* Now the deployed
   tree has a verdict of its own. But the last green sha is behind the tip by
   however many merges landed during the run, so "deploy main" does not mean
   deploy main — it means deploy a main from some minutes ago. Whether that is
   acceptable is exactly guard 4b's classification question: =inert= and
   =pipeline=, fine; =artifact= and =hotfix=, not fine. The guard is needed in
   full.

3. *Validate main post-merge, deploy the tip.* The tip is by construction the
   sha whose run has not finished. Guard 2's C2 requirement — a verdict bound
   to *this* hash — is unmet at the moment of reliance.

So the honest statement: *mainline-only relocates guard 4b's subject from a
branch to a sha, and leaves the predicate intact.* What it genuinely kills is
guard 0 and a whole class of "which tree is this?" questions — including the
worst one, which is a production incident where the running artifact
corresponds to no commit anybody can find. That is not nothing. It is just not
the 1/20.

The only way to close the gap in arrangement 1 is to validate every merge
commit before it becomes the tip — that is, one validation run per merge. Which
is exactly per-change cost, and exactly what a *merge queue* is. A merge queue
that batches speculatively is a release train relocated from the deploy point
to the merge point; the amortization arithmetic below is the same arithmetic.

* The amortization analysis

** The direct answer to 1/20

ADR-0001's row is reproduced first, so the comparison is against the same
engine and not against a paraphrase of it. [E]

#+begin_example
== 1. ADR-0001 baseline reproduced (one shot per change, 20 artifact) ==
| berths | completed | forfeits |
|      1 |  1/20     |       19 |
|      4 |  1/20     |       16 |
|      8 |  1/20     |       12 |

== 1b. The same 20 changes on ONE train ==
| one train | 20/20 completed | 1 validation run | all land in a single merge to trunk |
#+end_example

Twenty validation cycles and nineteen forfeits become one validation cycle and
zero forfeits. That is the claim, and it is trivially true once stated: the
1/20 is caused by *each merge invalidating the other nineteen*, and a batch has
one merge.

** But 1/20 is a transient, and the honest baseline is worse

ADR-0001's harness runs each change once: forfeit and you are done. A human
would rebase, re-gate and re-book. Doing that turns 1/20 into eventual
completion, so the interesting quantity is not completions-out-of-twenty but
*validation runs consumed per shipped change*, measured under an open arrival
stream rather than a single burst. [E]

This *refines* ADR-0001 rather than contradicting it, and the refinement is
worth recording because the ADR's phrasing invites an overstatement.

- Berths are not literally worthless: at 24 offered/day, going 1 → 16 berths
  moves completions from 2.9 to 15.8 per day. ADR-0001's "adding berths moves
  completions from 1 to 1" is true of the *one-shot transient* it measured, and
  reading it as a general statement about capacity would be wrong.
- Berths buy *nothing at all* on the quantity that matters. Validation runs per
  shipped change is ~20 at every berth count tested, and ~68 at 48 offered/day
  at every berth count tested. *Capacity does not reduce revalidation work; it
  only lets more of it happen in parallel.* That is ADR-0001's real finding and
  it survives the refinement intact.

Mean wait is half the cadence, as expected, and the simulator agrees with the
closed form to within the queueing delay of the path itself. [E]

** Debugging a red train: the crossing point

The other cost is attribution. A red train tells you the *batch* is bad and
nothing about which member. The model charges =ceil(log2 N)= validation runs to
isolate one bad car plus one run to revalidate the remainder — which is exactly
what =spec.org='s "one logical step per commit" discipline is buying: it makes
each bisect step cheap to produce and its verdict meaningful.

Expected changes shipped per validation run, =N= cars, per-change failure
probability =p=: [I]

: yield(N, p) = (N − Np) / (1 + Np·(ceil(log2 N) + 1))

*Yes, there is a crossing, and it has a clean closed form.* [I] The optimum
sits at =N*·p ≈ 0.6–0.8= across two orders of magnitude of =p= — that is, the
throughput-optimal train is sized to carry *slightly under one broken change*.
Equivalently, and much less comfortably:

#+begin_quote
=P(green train)= at the optimum is *0.41–0.53*. The revalidation-optimal
release train is red slightly more often than it is green.
#+end_quote

That is a real result and it is not an argument for running trains that way.
It is throughput-optimal and nothing else: it is not latency-optimal, and a
batch that is red more than half the time is a batch where more than half of
the people on it are waiting for someone else's defect. §The balance's test
applies — name what it protects, name who pays — and the answer here is that
production gains nothing from the fifteenth car and fourteen people pay for
the sixteenth.

Note also the degenerate row. At =p = 0= the optimal batch is unbounded, which
says only "if nothing ever fails, never validate twice". Any argument for large
trains that does not carry a failure rate is that row in disguise.

** The negative result that matters

The optimum is unreachable, and this repo's own model says so. Feeding the
measured train latencies into =gates/simulate-gates.py='s =emergency_pressure=
curve (§11 above): at =p = 0.05= the analytic optimum is =N* = 16=, reached at
a cadence of 8–16 hours, where the predicted emergency share is *92–99%*.

*A cadence long enough to amortize well is a cadence long enough to push
essentially all traffic onto the one path that skips staging.* [E, given the
logistic in =simulate-gates.py=, which is itself a model and not an
observation — so this is [E] about the two models agreeing, and [H] about the
world.]

The constructive half of the same table is the useful one:

- A *one-hour* train already gets =0.58= validations/ship against per-change's
  =13.63=. The first two cars capture ~98% of the available amortization,
  because the baseline is so bad.
- Going from a one-hour to a twenty-four-hour cadence improves
  validations/ship from =0.58= to =0.32= — a factor of 1.8 — while taking the
  predicted emergency share from 23% to 100%.

*If a train is ever adopted here, run it short.* The marginal return on cadence
is small and the marginal cost is the bypass. §History reaches the same place
from an entirely different direction: Facebook's replacement for the weekly
branch was not per-commit deployment but "tens to hundreds of diffs every few
hours", and Chrome has compressed its train from six weeks to two rather than
abolishing it.

** Bisect is not optional

"Roll the train back and everyone tries again" is not a recovery, it is a
deferral: somebody still has to find out which car broke it. Modelling that as
"dissolve and revalidate every car individually" — per-change cost paid all at
once — gives: [E]

#+begin_example
== 8. Bisect vs dissolve: what attribution is worth (cadence 8 slots = 4 h) ==
| p_bad | bisect/day | dissolve/day | bisect val/ship | dissolve val/ship | bisect shipped nothing | dissolve shipped nothing |
|  0.02 |       23.7 |         23.7 |            0.32 |              0.35 |  0/24 |  0/24 |
|  0.05 |       23.7 |         23.6 |            0.41 |              0.51 |  0/24 |  0/24 |
|  0.10 |       23.7 |         13.0 |            0.58 |              2.48 |  0/24 |  2/24 |
|  0.20 |       23.3 |          1.5 |            1.04 |             31.45 |  0/24 |  8/24 |
#+end_example

At =p = 0.20= the no-attribution train *livelocks*: throughput collapses from
23.3 to 1.5 per day and a third of the ten-day runs ship nothing at all. The
gap between the two columns is precisely the value of the commit discipline —
=log2 N= versus =N=. A team that batches without being able to bisect has
bought batch latency and kept per-change cost.

The =ceil(log2 N)= cost model is *this note's own inference* and is marked [H]
accordingly — see §Batch size and attribution cost, where the search for a
published source joining =git bisect='s binary search to the small-batch
argument came up empty. What /is/ documented first-party is the underlying
problem the model prices: "Any of the N changelists between the P and F may
have caused the breakage… we have no way to pinpoint the cause of a breakage or
fix" (Google, ICSE-SEIP 2017), and Facebook/OANDA's natural experiment finding
that batches of thousands of changes made "failures identified at deployment
time significantly harder to isolate" (ICSE'16).

* Intent is not evidence

This is the central point and it defeats both strategies for the case that
matters.

** The distinction is already in the spec

=spec.org= §Domain model states it twice, about two other things, and both
statements transfer without modification:

- *"manifest is derived, never asserted... If it can be asserted, it is not a
  manifest — it is a wish, and the build ships whatever someone typed."*
- *"deployment record is attested, not self-reported. The difference is who
  says it happened: the platform, or the job that wanted it to."*

A merge to main is an *assertion*. "On main" means some person, holding a
green verdict on a different tree, believed the combination was ready. It is
the same shape as a self-reported deployment record and the same shape as a
hand-edited manifest: a statement by the party that wanted it to be true.

In capability terms the point is sharp. "On main" is *C1* — mutable state on a
change, and in this case not even owned by automation. It is not *C2* — a
verdict bound to a content hash. The merge commit is a content hash that
usually has no verdict at all. And =spec.org= already names this exact failure
and calls it silent:

#+begin_quote
"bind a verdict to a branch or a change id instead of a hash and everything
still appears to work, right up until the head moves and a stale pass
authorizes a deploy."
#+end_quote

*"Deploy main" is a branch-bound authorization.* It is C2's named failure mode
lifted from the gate step to the deployment step. Mainline-only is therefore
not merely silent about guard 4b — stated carelessly, it re-introduces the
thing C2 exists to forbid. The disciplined form is "deploy =main@<sha>= where
=<sha>= carries its own verdict", and that form costs one validation per merge,
which is the cost the train was supposed to amortize away.

** What each strategy actually does to C5

C5 — verdicts revocable after issue — is one of the two capabilities
=spec.org= marks *rare*, and it is guard 4b's. Neither strategy removes the
need for it. What a train changes is its *economics*: when a hotfix lands under
a forming branch, there is one verdict to revoke and one run to re-earn it,
where per-change mode revokes N and re-runs N. [E]

#+begin_example
== 10. How often does break-glass void the batch? (cadence 4 h, offered 24/day) ==
| emergencies/day | trains rebuilt | rebuild rate | validations/ship | mean latency (h) |
|               0 |            0.0 |         0.00 |             0.25 |              3.3 |
|               1 |            2.5 |         0.04 |             0.26 |              3.3 |
|               2 |            4.5 |         0.08 |             0.27 |              3.3 |
|               4 |           10.5 |         0.18 |             0.30 |              3.3 |
|               8 |           22.3 |         0.38 |             0.35 |              3.4 |
#+end_example

At eight emergencies a day, 38% of trains are rebuilt and the cost is +0.10
validations per shipped change. The train absorbs emergencies *cheaply*. It
does not absorb them *at all* in the sense that matters, because the emergency
never rides.

** A train that can be jumped is not a train for the case that matters

By construction, an emergency cannot wait for the cadence — mean wait is =C/2=,
and an emergency that waits two hours was not an emergency. So every train has
a bypass, and the bypass is exactly where intent and evidence diverge most:

- =spec.org= §Domain model, *authorization*: normal is "earned by gates",
  emergency is "granted by a person". The intent/evidence split is in the
  spec's own table, as the definition of the two authorization kinds.
- An emergency PR is *exempt from guard 0*, so it may be behind main.
- It bypasses guard 4 — staging — which is the only gate that tests the
  *combination*.
- What is left is guard 2 (gates green on the head sha) and guard 5 (the
  served build), which is precisely why =spec.org= refuses to let emergency
  bypass either. Those two are the only evidence in the transaction; everything
  else is intent.

A release train changes none of this. Neither does mainline-only: the
emergency merges to main, and main now carries a tree that composes with the
forming batch in a way nobody validated. The train's response — rebuild the
batch — is the *right* response and is cheap (above), but it is a response to
having been jumped, not a way of not being jumped.

Stated plainly, which is what this section exists for:

| Question | Release trains | Mainline-only |
|----------+----------------+---------------|
| Does it reduce guard 4b's *cost*? | *Yes*, by a factor of N. 13.63 → 0.58 validations/ship measured [E] | *No.* Neutral at best; naive form re-introduces C2's failure |
| Does it reduce guard 4b's *necessity*? | *No.* One subject instead of N, same predicate, same rare C4/C5 | *No.* Same predicate, relocated from a branch to a sha |
| Does it make a merge into evidence? | *No.* It makes the batch's merge into evidence, which is more than today — but the merge itself is still an assertion until a run covers it | *No.* "On main" stays C1 |
| Does it help =itil:emergency=? | *No.* The emergency jumps by construction; the batch evidence never covers it | *No.* Same |
| Does it make emergencies *cheaper*? | *Yes*: 1 revocation and 1 rebuild for the batch, +0.10 validations/ship at 8/day [E] | *Marginally*: the revert target is nameable |

* Services deployed continuously over time

This is where the notes matter most, and where the repo's guards are weakest —
because guard 5 as written assumes an atomic cutover, and a long-lived service
with rolling deploys does not have one.

** Guard 5 is false by construction during a roll

: 5 | served_build(production) = H(c) | after deploy

=gates/health.sh= implements this by issuing one request per route and
comparing one =x-build-sha= header against the expected sha. During a rolling
deploy, some replicas serve the previous sha and some serve =H(c)=, so:

- the predicate is *false for part of the fleet by design*, not by fault;
- the check *samples* — it reads one replica's answer and generalizes;
- so it will flap, and a flapping safety check is one people learn to re-run.

The restatement a rolling deploy needs is a *different predicate*, not a
relaxed one: [I]

: during the roll:   ∀ responses : served_build ∈ {prev, H(c)}    -- and nothing else
: after deadline D:  ∀ replicas  : served_build = H(c)

The first clause is the one that catches real incidents — a third sha in the
set means an earlier deploy never finished — and it cannot be expressed at all
by a guard that compares against a single expected value.

** "The deployed version" is not one thing, so the record must say so

C6 — a platform-attested deployment record — is the capability guard 4 rests
on. Under rolling deploys, "artifact X reached environment Y" is ambiguous for
the duration of the roll, which is exactly the window in which somebody asks.
A record that carries only a sha cannot answer "was the hotfix live at 14:12?".
It needs the sha *and* a convergence interval. [I]

This is the same attested-versus-self-reported distinction again: a deploy job
that writes "deployed" when it finishes *starting* the roll is self-reporting
an event that has not happened yet.

** Guard 4b needs a third evaluation point

=spec.org= already moved guard 4b from request time to activation, and
Hypothesis then moved it again to the merge — "activation is not the last point
of reliance; the merge is." A rolling deploy adds one more: if main moves with
a =hotfix=-class merge *twenty minutes into a roll*, every replica not yet
converged is about to be handed a tree that reverts it. The last point of
reliance is not the start of the roll, and for a long roll it is not the merge
either. [H] — the simulator has no rolling model, so this is reasoning from the
guard's own rule, not an observation.

** Rollback targets, and the one place the strategies compose

A rollback target must be *reachable* and it must have *evidence*. The two
strategies split these:

| | Rollback target is nameable? | Does it have a verdict? | Granularity |
|--+------------------------------+-------------------------+-------------|
| Per-change, branch deploys | often not — the running artifact may correspond to no long-lived ref | pre-merge verdict on a different tree | one change |
| Mainline-only | *yes* — a main sha | pre-merge only, unless merge commits are validated | one merge |
| Release train | yes — the train's merge commit | *yes* — the batch verdict describes exactly the tree that shipped | *N changes* |
| Mainline-only + trains | yes | yes | N changes |

The last row is the only genuine composition in this note. It is also where the
train's real cost under continuous deployment shows: *you cannot roll back one
car.* Reverting a single change out of a landed train produces a tree that was
never validated — a new change needing its own run, during an incident, which
is the worst possible moment to be creating untested trees. The train trades
rollback *precision* for rollback *evidence*, and for a service that is
deployed continuously that trade is load-bearing in both directions.

* History — what is verifiable

The prompt for these notes recalled Facebook's release engineering team
discussing this around 2011: Chuck Rossi, trunk plus a weekly release branch
plus cherry-picks, "push karma", daily pushes. That recollection is *largely
correct*, and the places where it is wrong or incomplete are more useful than
the places where it is right. Everything below was checked; anything that
could not be checked is marked as such rather than repeated.

Method note: =engineering.fb.com= refused direct connections from hydra;
those pages were read through a text-extraction proxy and cross-checked
against independent secondary sources. Where a claim rests only on a secondary
source, it says so.

** Verified

*** Rossi, and the talks [E]

Chuck Rossi was Facebook's first release engineer, hired 2008, later
engineering director of Release Engineering — stated on Facebook's own blog,
/Release engineering and push karma: Chuck Rossi/, 2012-04-05,
=engineering.fb.com/2012/04/05/production-engineering/release-engineering-and-push-karma-chuck-rossi/=.

The 2011 date in the recollection checks out: the internal tech talk /Pushing
Millions of Lines of Code Five Days a Week/ was given 2011-05-26; the surviving
public record is a contemporaneous third-party writeup (Lester Chan,
2011-06-03, =lesterchan.net=), not a Facebook publication. Verified
first-party talks include QCon SF 2012 "Release Engineering at Facebook";
RELENG 2014 (2nd International Workshop on Release Engineering, 2014-04-11),
listed on the program as an *invited talk*, not a keynote; USENIX SREcon14
"Releasing at Scale" (panel); and @Scale 2017 "Rapid release at massive scale"
(2017-09-12).

Two corrections worth carrying, because both are widely miscited:

- The much-linked QCon SF 2013 / InfoQ recording "The Facebook Mobile Release
  Process" is *Christian Legnitto's*, not Rossi's.
- *No Velocity talk by Rossi could be verified.* [unverified]

*** The model [E]

Facebook's own retrospective states it exactly, and is the citation to use:

#+begin_quote
"For many years, we pushed the Facebook front end three times a day using a
simple master and release branch strategy. Engineers would request
cherry-picks — changes to the code that had passed a series of automated tests
— to pull from the master branch into one of the daily pushes from the release
branch." … "Once a week, we'd cut a new release branch that picked up any
changes that were not cherry-picked during the week."

— /Rapid release at massive scale/, Facebook Engineering Blog, 2017-08-31
#+end_quote

The Sunday cut is right for the web tier circa 2011 — "cut from trunk to RC on
Sunday 6pm", weekly push "Tuesday 4pm" — but rests on the contemporaneous
third-party writeup above, corroborated for cadence by Ars Technica
(Ryan Paul, 2012-04-05: "a minor update on every single business day. Major
updates… once a week, generally on Tuesday afternoons"). For *mobile* the same
facts are in a peer-reviewed paper and are exact: "For iOS, a Release branch is
cut from the Master branch by RelEng at a two week cadence every other Sunday
at 6pm"; Android at a one-week cadence with a Thursday code freeze; and the cut
date was moved from Thursday to Sunday in August 2015 (Android) and February
2016 (iOS) — Rossi, Shibley, Su, Beck, Savor, Stumm, /Continuous Deployment of
Mobile Software at Facebook/, FSE'16, pp. 12–23, DOI 10.1145/2950290.2994157.

"Daily pushes" is a moving target and the recollection fixes it too early: one
per day (2011) → two per day (Rossi, /Ship early and ship twice as often/,
2012-08-03) → three per day (by 2016) → "tens to hundreds of diffs every few
hours" (2017).

*** Push karma [E]

The term is verified, and it is Facebook's own — it is in the *title* of the
2012-04-05 post. Rossi, verbatim:

#+begin_quote
"Everyone is born with 4 stars, and you can only go down from there. People
with low push karma have a higher bar to get into the daily push."
#+end_quote

One refinement on the recollection: the documented mechanism is that low karma
*raises the bar for getting your cherry-pick into the push*, and karma degrades
when an engineer fails to be available to *support* a change during the push —
not only when a change breaks. Corroborated in an interview (Willie Wheeler,
2012-12-02/06: "Developers with a history of pushing garbage through get more
scrutiny. They track this, though any given developer's push karma isn't
public.").

*** Scale [E]

From the 2017-08-31 post: "between 500 and 700 cherry-picks per day"; "We were
ingesting more than 1,000 diffs a day to the master branch"; "the weekly push
was sometimes as many as 10,000 diffs". FSE'16 adds that mobile RelEng "has
fewer than 10 members" while mobile developers went from "fewer than 100" in
2012 to "over 1,500" in 2016.

*** Why the branch model was revised [E]

Facebook's stated reason is a *coordination* cost, not a correctness one:

#+begin_quote
"By 2016, we saw that the branch/cherry-pick model was reaching its limit." …
"The amount of manual effort needed to coordinate and deliver such a large
release every week was not sustainable."
#+end_quote

The replacement is a *quasi-continuous* push from master: "tens to hundreds of
diffs every few hours", rolled out over a year from April 2016 to 100% of
production web servers in April 2017, staged employees → 2% of production →
100%, with push-blocking alerts, an emergency stop button, =Gatekeeper= feature
flags decoupling code release from feature release, and =Flytrap= aggregating
user reports. (Gatekeeper and Flytrap are independently attested in FSE'16.)

** Where the practice has been revised, and where it has not

*The most important correction to the recollection is that "Facebook abandoned
the release train in 2017" is true only of the web tier.* The same 2017 post
says, verbatim: "Today we use the same kind of branch/cherry-pick model on
mobile that we previously used on web." Mobile compressed the train — four
weeks → two → one for Android — and *kept* the branch-and-cherry-pick model.
Collapsing web and mobile is the single most common error in retellings of this
history, and it inverts the conclusion.

Two findings from FSE'16 are directly relevant and cut against the easy story:

- *Finding 4:* "The length of the deployment cycle does not significantly
  affect the relative number of cherry-picks and launch-blockers as it
  decreases from 4 weeks to 2 weeks (and then to 1 week for Android)."
- *Finding 5:* "The shortening of the release cycle does not appear to affect
  the crash rate."
- *Finding 6*, which is the one that did move: "Software pushed on the day of
  the deadline is of lower quality", with the remedy being a shorter cycle
  ("developers were less likely to rush their software pushes, knowing that
  another release was upcoming shortly") and moving the cut to a weekend.

So the measured benefit of shortening the train was *not* fewer defects per
change and *not* fewer cherry-picks. It was the removal of a deadline that
people crammed against. That is a narrower claim than "small batches are
better", and this note should not claim more than it.

** What the broader literature says, including where it contradicts the note above

*** Trunk-based development — the recollection's "cherry-picking is a smell" is false [E]

A case-insensitive search of all 30 pages of =trunkbaseddevelopment.com=
(Paul Hammant) found *zero* occurrences of "smell", "anti-pattern" or
"antipattern". The site prescribes cherry-picking as correct practice for
lower-cadence teams — "The best practice… is to reproduce the bug on the
trunk, fix it there with a test… then cherry-pick that to the release branch" —
and condemns only the *reverse* direction ("if you are fixing bugs on the
release branch and merging them down to the trunk you are doing it wrong") and
long-lived release branches. Its actual position is cadence-dependent: "Teams
with a higher release cadence [release directly from trunk], and those with a
lower release cadence [branch for release]."

The defensible version of the recollection is Hammant's own, on his blog rather
than the site: "The CD-style firehose into production from trunk, should be
your goal though. i.e. no branches." (2015-12-13).

*** Accelerate / DORA — supports small batches, and is silent on release trains [E]

This is the strongest contradiction to how these notes might otherwise be
argued. Full-text search of the 2016 and 2017 State of DevOps Report PDFs for
"release branch", "release train" and "cherry" returns *zero matches in each*.
*DORA never studied release trains.* What it measured, verbatim (2016 report,
p. 31):

#+begin_quote
"We found that having branches or forks with very short lifetimes (less than a
day) before being merged into trunk, and less than three active branches in
total, are important aspects of continuous delivery… Teams that don't have code
freeze periods (when people can't merge code or pull requests) also achieve
higher performance."
#+end_quote

And DORA's current guidance explicitly *accommodates* the branch model: "there
are cases where bug fixes must be cherry picked and merged into releases… In
cases where releases happen multiple times a day, release branches are not
required at all" (=dora.dev/capabilities/trunk-based-development/=).

Two consequences for this note, and both are constraints on it:

1. Anyone citing Accelerate as a verdict *on release trains* is over-reading
   it. These notes do not cite it that way.
2. What DORA does penalize with data — branch lifetime over a day, more than
   three active branches, code-freeze periods — maps onto a *long-cadence*
   train, whose stabilization window is a code freeze by DORA's definition.
   *That mapping is inference, not a source statement* [I], and it points the
   same way as §The negative result that matters: run it short, or not at all.

*** Google — endorses branch-and-cherry-pick, for a reason this note should take seriously [E]

/Release Engineering/, ch. 8 of /Site Reliability Engineering/ (O'Reilly 2016),
*written by Dinah McNutt* — not by the book's editors, who are frequently
miscredited. Verbatim, and this is the passage that matters:

#+begin_quote
"All code is checked into the main branch of the source code tree (mainline).
However, most major projects don't release directly from the mainline.
Instead, we branch from the mainline at a specific revision and never merge
changes from the branch back into the mainline. Bug fixes are submitted to the
mainline and then cherry picked into the branch for inclusion in the release.
This practice avoids inadvertently picking up unrelated changes submitted to
the mainline since the original build occurred. *Using this branch and cherry
pick method, we know the exact contents of each release.*"
#+end_quote

The widely-quoted sentence "virtually no releases are built directly from the
mainline" *does not appear in that chapter* — do not quote it.

The italicised clause is the interesting one for §Intent is not evidence. Google's
stated reason for branching is not throughput and not batching economics: it is
that the branch makes the release's contents *knowable*, where the mainline tip
does not. That is the same distinction these notes draw between C1 and C2, made
by a team that reached the opposite operational conclusion. It is an awkward
witness for release trains and an excellent one for the claim that "deploy main"
is under-specified.

Chrome is where the train metaphor comes from, and it is evidence *for* trains:
"We basically wanted to operate more like trains leaving Grand Central Station
(regularly scheduled and always on time)… if a given feature is not complete, it
will simply ride on the the next release train" (Anthony Laforge, Chromium Blog,
2010-07-22). The cadence has compressed 6 weeks → 4 weeks (2021-03-04) → 2
weeks (announced 2026-03-03, effective September 2026), with Google's own
stated reason being that "their smaller scope minimizes disruption and
simplifies post-release debugging". *The industry trend is shorter trains, not
the abolition of scheduled cuts* — which is exactly what §The negative result
that matters concludes from the simulator, by a different route.

Chromium's own documentation prices the cherry-pick the way this note prices
revalidation: "merging (also known as cherry-picking) code to an older release
branch introduces risk and costs time across the team", and "Merge criteria
become more strict as the stable release date approaches"
(=docs/process/merge_request.md=). Android keeps the same structure: "Google
might cherry pick changes into this release branch to address bug fixes"
(AOSP /Release lifecycle/).

*** Batch size and attribution cost [E], with one gap [H]

Reinertsen, /The Principles of Product Development Flow/ (Celeritas, 2009),
ch. 5 *"Reducing Batch Size"* — note the chapter title, which is often given
wrongly — contains two principles that are precisely §Debugging a red train:

- *B10, The Least Common Denominator Principle of Batch Size:* "The entire
  batch is limited by its worst element." (p. 119)
- *B11, The Principle of Batch Size Economics:* "Economic batch size is a
  U-curve optimization." (p. 121)

B11 is the same shape as the =yield(N, p)= curve above, arrived at from
queueing theory rather than from this repo's guards. That is corroboration
worth having; it is also a reminder that the U-curve is the expected result and
the *interesting* finding here is where the optimum sits relative to the
emergency-pressure curve, not that an optimum exists.

For the cost of diagnosing a batched failure there are two good first-party
citations:

- Savor, Douglas, Gentili, Williams, Beck, Stumm, /Continuous Deployment at
  Facebook and OANDA/, ICSE'16 Companion, pp. 21–30, DOI
  10.1145/2889160.2889223 — a natural experiment in which the *same codebase*
  was deployed continuously internally and in large batches to white-label
  customers whose policies forbade CD: "each deployment in the white-labeled
  product incorporated thousands of changes… *failures identified at deployment
  time were significantly harder to isolate to identify their root cause*.
  Developers had mentally moved on to work on software several generations
  later."
- Memon et al., /Taming Google-Scale Continuous Testing/, ICSE-SEIP 2017 — the
  red-train attribution problem stated first-party: "A milestone is typically
  cut every 45 minutes… *Any of the N changelists between the P and F may have
  caused the breakage*", and "because TAP does not run each affected target at
  each CL, we have no way to pinpoint the cause of a breakage or fix."

*The gap, stated honestly:* no credible published source was found that builds
the small-batch argument on =git bisect='s =O(log n)= property. The binary
search is documented in =git-bisect(1)=; the N-way ambiguity is documented by
Memon et al.; *joining them is this note's own inference* and the
=ceil(log2 N)= cost model in §Debugging a red train is therefore [H], not
borrowed. If it is wrong, it is wrong here and not upstream.

- The byline of the 2017-08-31 post. The page carries *no byline*; attribution

- "4,000–6,000 changes in the weekly Tuesday release" — single secondary source

** What the history changes about the argument above

Three things, and none of them is decorative.

1. *The measured reason for abandoning the weekly branch was coordination
   cost, not correctness* — 500–700 cherry-picks a day is a full-time human
   dispatcher, and "push karma" is what a dispatcher builds when the queue
   exceeds their capacity to judge each item on its merits. That is
   §Intent is not evidence in its purest historical form: the gate was a
   release engineer's judgement about risk, explicitly "a judgement call by
   RelEng involving a trade-off between risk and impact" (FSE'16). It was
   intent all the way down, and it worked for years, and it stopped scaling at
   roughly 1,000 diffs a day.
2. *The replacement is a short train, not the absence of one.* "Tens to
   hundreds of diffs every few hours" is a batch with a cadence. Chrome
   compressed 6 → 4 → 2 weeks. Mobile compressed 4 → 2 → 1 weeks and kept
   cherry-picks. Nobody in this record reached per-commit deployment of a
   shared artifact surface. That is independent support for §The negative
   result that matters' conclusion — the useful move is a *short* train — and
   it is support arrived at by an entirely different route.
3. *Google's stated reason for branching is the one these notes care about.*
   "Using this branch and cherry pick method, we know the exact contents of
   each release" is a claim about *evidence*, not throughput. A team that
   cannot say what is in a release cannot revoke a verdict on it (C5) and
   cannot name a rollback target (§Services deployed over time). That argument
   survives regardless of which strategy anyone picks.

The last row is the reason this file is notes and not a proposal. A release
train is a *very good* repricing of the shared-surface contention — a 23×
reduction in revalidation work is not a rounding error — and it is still a
repricing. =spec.org= §What this model gets wrong, on purpose applies to it
without modification: the train, like the berth and the queue and guard 4b, is
machinery for surviving a missing contract. A reader who takes the train home
without noticing that has taken home the wrong artifact.

§History supplies the empirical version of the same warning. Facebook ran the
branch-and-cherry-pick train for years and abandoned it for the web when the
*coordination* cost — 500–700 cherry-picks a day, dispatched by hand, with
"push karma" as the rationing device — stopped scaling. It was never the
batching arithmetic that failed. It was that a human was standing where a
contract should have been.

* Refutation conditions

What would falsify the claims above, in the repo's house style:

1. A per-change run under open arrivals that achieves under 2 validation runs
   per shipped change at 24 offered/day on an all-=artifact= mix. Would refute
   "capacity does not reduce revalidation work".
2. A train mode whose measured optimum has =N*·p= outside =[0.4, 1.0]= for
   =p ∈ [0.01, 0.33]=. Would refute the closed form in §Debugging a red train.
3. A statement of mainline-only under which guard 4b's predicate is
   *unstateable* rather than merely relocated — as Option A makes it. Would
   refute §Mainline-only does not touch guard 4b.
4. A deployment arrangement in which an emergency rides the train and still
   reaches production within the emergency's own deadline. Would refute
   §A train that can be jumped.
5. Any observed system where "merged to main" is itself a platform-attested
   verdict on the merge commit's content hash, produced without a run. Would
   refute §Intent is not evidence, and would be very interesting.
