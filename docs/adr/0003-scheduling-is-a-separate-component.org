#+TITLE: ADR 0003 — the change schedule is a separate component, deliberately kept dumb
#+DATE: 2026-09-13

* Status

Accepted.

* Context

The change schedule decides *when* a change may take the one path to
production. There is a very large literature on schedulers — priorities, fair
queueing, backfill, deadline scheduling, preemption — and almost none of it
applies here, because the resource is not scarce in the way those solve for.

What we actually needed, and now have, is three verbs:

- =block <pr> <groups> <minutes>= — take the next free aligned slot behind
  whatever is already reserved.
- =block ... --at <iso>= — take a *named* slot.
- =reap.sh= — close reservations whose time has passed.

That is the whole scheduler. It is first-fit with an explicit override.

* Decision

*Scheduling rules stay out of the deployment workflow.* The schedule answers
one question — does an open reservation cover now — and the guards do not ask
it anything else. No priority, no preemption, no automatic reslotting, no
backfill of gaps.

If richer scheduling is ever wanted it is *a separate component* that produces
reservations, and the pipeline keeps reading the same one bit. Nothing below
=schedule.sh= should learn what a priority is.

* Why

** The scarce resource is a person, not staging

A named hour is usually a developer saying *"I will be at my desk then and I
want to watch this go out."* That is what makes automatic reslotting wrong
rather than merely rude: moving the change moves it away from the person who
arranged to be present, and the value of the slot was the attention, not the
minutes.

So =--at= does not win a clash, and gaps in the calendar are correct. A
scheduler that packed them would be optimising utilisation of a resource that
is not scarce (staging) at the cost of one that is.

** Every rule is a rule that can be wrong at 3am

Each of the guards has cost us a real defect this project: guard 2 had two
implementations that disagreed, guard 3 reported a path error as "no window",
=schedule.sh current= still returns 0 for an expired window. Those are simple
rules and they were still wrong. A priority scheme would be more rules of the
same kind, in the component that decides who gets production.

** The interesting failures are in expiry and rebooking, not in placement

What the grind actually surfaced was not "the wrong change went first". It was:
a window that lapsed and was never closed kept answering *yes, you have a
window*; an un-reaped lapse pushed the whole queue out behind a reservation
nobody was using; a five-minute slot could not hold a five-minute soak plus the
walk. None of those are placement problems, and a better placement algorithm
would not have found any of them.

* The API is three verbs, and that is the whole point

: block   <change> <groups> <minutes> [--at <instant>] [--short]
: windows <change> [env]
: reap

Everything else — =current=, =check=, =close=, =list=, =freeze= — already
existed for the guards to read. Placement is *one rule*: after the latest end
among open windows, or now, whichever is later. No modes, no gap-filling, no
priority.

Two cleverer versions were tried on 2026-09-13 and both were worse:

- *First wide-enough gap from now.* A cancellation opens a hole and the next
  booking drops into it, ahead of changes that queued first. Queue-jumping, and
  invisible — both bookings look correct in isolation.
- *Distinguish queued from designated placement.* Needs a field every existing
  window lacks, so they all default to one mode and the bug returns wearing a
  flag.

The known cost is accepted: *a far-future designated slot pushes later
auto-bookings past it*. Booking a release for Saturday moved two ten-minute
changes from tonight into next weekend. The fix is =--at=, which is a person
deciding — which is the arrangement we wanted.

*Predictable beats optimal here.* A person owns scheduling; the scheduler's job
is to not surprise them.

** Duration is the one thing that is not a person's guess

=block= refuses a window that cannot hold the work: deploy and settle 3m + the
soak + 1m per extra app + 10m for control-plane, derived from =SOAK_SECONDS= so
changing the hold moves the floor. A 5m slot cannot hold a 5m soak, and an
afternoon of bookings lapsed at exactly the point the hold ended before this
existed. =--short= overrides, loudly.

* Consequences

- The queue is FIFO with holes. Anyone who wants a particular hour asks for it.
- A designated slot that clashes is *refused*, and moving the other change is a
  human act recorded as a cancellation — never a silent displacement.
- Expiry is a first-class outcome (=expired=, distinct from =failed= and
  =cancelled=), and =reap.sh= is the only thing that produces it.
- =reap.sh= deliberately does **not** rebook. A reaper that rebooks is a
  scheduler nobody asked for, and it would hide the signal worth seeing: that
  the slots are too short for the work they hold.
- The simulator carries =BOOKING= as an axis (=none= / =queued= / =designated=)
  and asserts the two booking modes are *indistinguishable at the point of
  deploying* — if =designated= could reach an environment where =queued= could
  not, =--at= would be a bypass wearing a calendar's clothes.

* What the local calendar is for

=refs/idp/schedule= is a *stand-in*, kept here for two reasons only:

- *testing* — the semantics (windows, clashes, closure codes, expiry, the
  berth) need something executable to be exercised against, and every finding
  in =scenarios.org= came from driving a real one.
- *visibility* — the dashboard can show a queue, which is how the expired
  window, the stale reservation and the five unclassified changes became
  obvious rather than theoretical.

*It is not a proposal for where a real organisation keeps its change schedule.*
A real one lives where the team already works, and the CAS-on-a-git-ref
implementation is here because it was the cheapest thing that could be wrong in
interesting ways — not because a git ref is a good calendar.

Read everything below with that in mind: the *contract* is the artefact, the
store is a fixture.

* Explicit non-requirement: the forge does not know about the calendar

*GitHub has no concept of a deploy window, and we are not going to give it one.*

The change schedule can live anywhere a team already keeps one — a shared
calendar, Jira, ServiceNow, a spreadsheet, a git ref (which is what it is here).
A skill or a person books it. **Almost none of those are visible to GitHub**,
and nothing in branch protection, required checks, environments or rulesets can
express "only between 22:00 and 22:30".

So the forge cannot enforce the window, and we accept that rather than working
around it.

** What this means in practice

- =gates/preflight.sh= is the only thing that checks the window, and it is a
  *checklist a person runs*, not a gate the forge applies. Issue #51 records
  this with the trigger that would force a revisit.
- Auto-merge, the merge button, =workflow_dispatch= and anything calling a
  target directly all ignore the calendar completely. That is not a bug in
  those paths; it is the boundary.
- A change can therefore be merged, or deployed by a workflow, outside every
  window, and the schedule will not know. The record will show it — the window
  closes =expired= and the deployment record names a SHA no window covered — but
  *after the fact*, which is audit, not prevention.

** Why acknowledging beats bridging

The tempting fix is a bot that mirrors the calendar into a required check, so
the forge blocks a merge outside a window. We are not doing that:

- It puts the *authority* for "may this proceed" in a mirror, and the mirror is
  the thing most likely to be stale. A window that closed thirty seconds ago and
  a mirror that has not caught up are indistinguishable to the forge, and the
  answer it gives is the wrong one in the dangerous direction.
- It creates a second reader of the calendar. This repo has hit that defect
  twice already — guard 2 implemented in both =preflight= and =activate=, and
  the calendar read by both =preflight= and =schedule.sh current=, which still
  disagree about whether an expired window is open.
- It makes the forge's verdict a *cache* of a fact it cannot re-derive, which is
  precisely the shape every guard here is built to avoid.

An honest gap that everyone knows about is safer than a bridge that is right
most of the time. The calendar is the authority; the forge stores state and runs
tests, and it is not asked to adjudicate time.

* Rejected

- *Priority or preemption.* Nothing here is urgent except an emergency, and an
  emergency is already exempt from the freeze and the queue rules — but *not*
  from having a window, because the calendar is what tells everyone else the
  path is occupied.
- *Automatic reslotting on expiry.* See above; it destroys the signal and
  moves changes away from the people watching them.
- *Backfilling gaps.* The gaps are the design.
