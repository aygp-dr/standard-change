#+TITLE: ADR 0001 — automatic promotion to production, gated by evidence
#+DATE: 2026-09-13
#+OPTIONS: toc:2 num:nil

* Status

Accepted. *Supersedes ADR 0002*, which described the environment tiers as a
separate decision; they are a consequence of this one.

*Retitled 2026-09-13.* This was filed as "scheduled deployment windows with
separate activation", which named a mechanism and not a decision. The windows,
the berth, the tiers and the dashboard are all things this decision *needs*;
none of them is the thing being decided. An ADR titled after its mechanism
invites the next reader to argue about the mechanism.

Note also that *B is the fallback* — four cheaper options beat it on the number
that matters (§Decision rule). Recorded that way on purpose: an ADR presenting
its own decision as the answer, when the measurement says otherwise, is
advocacy rather than a record. Scope is the *workflow* — change enablement and the scheduling of
deployment windows. It is deliberately *not* about the activated deployment
itself: what a deployment does once it starts is deployment management's
concern and belongs in a separate decision.

* Context

Changes reach production through a single shared path (§What this model gets
wrong, on purpose). Today, holding the path and deploying are the same act: a
change takes the berth and immediately begins. Three problems follow.

1. *Nobody can plan.* A change cannot reserve capacity for later, so ITIL 4's
   change schedule has nothing to schedule — the calendar records what happened
   rather than governing what will.
2. *Guards are evaluated at request time.* Guard 4b exists because the world
   moves between asking and acting (=experiments/001=). Splitting the two acts
   gives that re-check a natural home.
3. *Throughput is opaque.* Without a reservation grid there is no way to say
   how many changes a day can absorb, so "add more capacity" is untestable.

The change schedule is a calendar, so windows quantize. At 30 minutes that is
*48 slots per day per berth*, and no gate improvement can buy a slot the
schedule does not have.

* Decision

#+begin_quote
*A change that passes its gates moves to production without a person moving
it.* People raise a change and, for a normal change, approve it. Nobody
performs the promotion.
#+end_quote

So there is no "push to staging" step and no "now push to production" step. A
change is promoted by *evidence arriving*: the authorizing run passes and
=staging:passed= appears; guard 2 is satisfied and =deploy:production= follows;
convergence is measured and =production:healthy= follows; the merge is
triggered by that. Each transition is caused by an observation, not a command
(§Labels as state transitions in =spec.org=).

*What a person still does*, and it is deliberately small:

| act                  | who    | why it cannot be automated              |
|----------------------+--------+-----------------------------------------|
| raise the change     | human  | it is the intent                        |
| approve =change:normal= | human | the gates cannot judge a control-plane change |
| break glass          | human  | =change:emergency= is an authorization, not a measurement |

Everything else is the system. *The approval gate sits before production, not
between staging and production* — a change that has earned staging has earned
production, because the same evidence governs both.

** The mechanism this requires

Automatic promotion is only safe if nothing can promote on stale evidence, and
that is what the rest of this ADR is about:

- *Reservation* (change enablement) — a change with green gates and an
  up-to-date base reserves a window on the change schedule. Reserving is not
  deploying. Guards 0 and 2 are checked here, for fast feedback.
- *Activation* (deployment management) — a background scheduler activates the
  reservation when its slot arrives, *re-evaluating every guard at that moment*.
  A change that is no longer eligible *forfeits the slot* rather than deploying.

Release management stays separate again: a release plans /what/ becomes
available and may exist long before any window is reserved.

* Consequences, measured

=sim/= is a closed-loop discrete-event simulator (Python + Hypothesis,
simulated time). It needs no pull requests, no forge, no running apps, no
worktrees and no network — deliberately, because those verify the port-block
and worktree rules, which are a different claim about a different system.

** It gives guard 4b its proper home

Reserve a window three hours out; an emergency breaks glass meanwhile:

#+begin_example
[day 0 00:00] CHG-0001 scheduled -> slot 6 (day 0 03:00)
[day 0 00:30] CHG-0002 ACTIVATED at slot 1
[day 0 01:00] CHG-0002 completed at slot 2; trunk -> 1
[day 0 03:00] CHG-0001 forfeits slot 6: trunk moved: HOTFIX (guard 4b)
#+end_example

"At reliance, not at request" stops being a rule we remember to apply and
becomes the thing the scheduler does.

** Hypothesis found two defects the worked example missed

- *Ordering within a quantum.* Activating before completing let a berth be
  released and retaken in the same slot, so the incoming change evaluated guard
  4b against a trunk that was about to move. D4's mechanism, reached through
  scheduling rather than emergency. Fixed: finish, then activate.
- *Guard 4b at the merge, not only at activation.* At =berths=2= two changes
  activate in the same slot, both pass (trunk has not moved), then both merge —
  the first moves trunk and the second ships a tree predating it. *Widening the
  berth reinstates D4* unless the merge is itself guarded. Activation is not the
  last point of reliance; the merge is.

** The finding that decides this ADR — corrected 2026-09-13

*An earlier revision of this ADR claimed that adding berths changes nothing:
1/20 completions at one berth and 1/20 at eight. That was wrong, and the error
was in the harness, not the model.* It reserved each change once and never let
a forfeited change rebase and re-book, so every forfeit was permanent. With
re-booking, berths do buy throughput:

| berths | one-shot (wrong) | re-booking (correct) | validations per ship |
|--------+------------------+----------------------+----------------------|
|      1 |             1/20 |                 3/20 |                 21.7 |
|      2 |             1/20 |                 6/20 |                 18.3 |
|      4 |             1/20 |                14/20 |                 13.9 |
|      8 |             1/20 |                20/20 |                 10.5 |
|     16 |             1/20 |                20/20 |                 10.5 |

What survives the correction — and is the sharper finding — is the last column.
*Every shipped change costs ten to twenty validation runs.* Adding berths
parallelizes that work; it does not reduce it. The shared artifact surface
means each merge invalidates the other scheduled changes, and they must
revalidate whether or not there is a berth free to revalidate in.

So the argument for Option A changes shape but not direction. It is *not* that
capacity cannot help — it can. It is that *the pipeline does an order of
magnitude more validation work than it ships changes*, and no amount of
capacity touches that ratio. Splitting the surface is what removes the
invalidation, and with it the ratio.

A release train attacks the same ratio from the other side by validating a
batch once — measured at *0.58 validations per ship against 13.63*, a 23×
reduction, at the cost of mean latency (2.6 h vs 12.5 h). See
=docs/release-strategies.org=; that note also finds the throughput-optimal
train is red more often than green, and that the analytic optimum sits at a
92–99% predicted emergency share on this repo's own balance curve — i.e.
unreachable. A short (≈1 h) train captures ~98% of the available amortization
and stays on the reachable part of the curve.

* Options considered

** Option A — do not build this; split the apps now  ← recommended if available

If the four apps can be given hard contracts at the router and a path to
production each, then: no shared surface, so no cross-invalidation; no queue,
so no berth; no guard 0, 1 or 4b; and scenario D4 becomes *unstateable* rather
than defended against. The table above is the argument — the all-inert row is
what every row looks like once changes stop colliding on one surface.

Cost: real engineering on contracts and per-team deploys, which is larger than
building a scheduler. Nothing else about this ADR is cheaper than that; it is
only *sooner*.

*Take this option if you can. The rest of this document is what to do when you
cannot.*

*** Problem — core is the default location, so core is on every request

Added 2026-09-13, on the same reasoning as =shared/= above: correct semantics,
worse fit for this decision, and named rather than hidden.

The estate now mirrors the nginx shape it is standing in for. An unclaimed path
is not a router-level 404 — it goes to whichever app declares =fallthrough= in
its =routes.json= (core), and *that app* decides the path does not exist:

: location /statics/  { proxy_pass http://core; }
: location /          { proxy_pass http://core; }   # was: return 404

*What it costs.* Two things, and the second is the one that matters here.

1. *Core is now in the blast radius of every request in the estate*, not just
   of =/=, =/login=, =/account=, =/cart= and the shared surface. A core that is
   down no longer degrades four journeys — it turns every unmatched path in the
   estate into a 502. Core has become infrastructure while still being labelled
   =app:core=, exactly the mismatch =shared/= introduced on the library side.
   The manifest still reports a single-app change.

2. *The gate that caught mis-routing could have been destroyed by this.* The
   e2e gate asserted "an unrouted path must 404 at the router". A default
   location makes that assertion impossible to satisfy, and the cheap move —
   delete the check — would have removed the only thing standing between us and
   a router that answers 200 for everything. It was rewritten instead to assert
   the *attribution*: 404, and =x-routed-to= names the fallthrough app. That is
   a strictly stronger check than the one it replaced, because it now also
   proves core is on the default path.

*It was nearly worse — and this one actually fired.* The first implementation
had core own any path matching the declared =/statics/= prefix. =/statics/=
being declared made =/statics/anything= return *200*. The default location had
silently become "core claims everything", which is the precise failure the
rewritten gate exists to catch — and the gate caught it, on the first run,
before commit. Ownership under =/statics/= is now decided by the file on disk,
not the prefix, and both halves are pinned: core answers for what it has, and
*refuses what it does not*. An app that cannot say no cannot be a default
location.

*Why do it anyway.* Because it is what the system being replicated does, and a
model of change control that only holds while every path is explicitly claimed
has not met a real storefront. The cost is that =app:core= now understates its
own blast radius twice over — once through =shared/=, once through the default
location. See the blast-radius follow-up below: this is the second independent
argument for =count(app:*) > 1= not being the right measure of radius.

*** Problem — shared/ makes automatic promotion less safe, and we did it anyway

Extracting =shared/= (OneUI, issue #10) and =external/= is *correct semantics*
and a *worse fit for this decision*. Both are true and the ADR should say so.

*What it costs.* Under automatic promotion, a change reaches production without
a person moving it. The gates are what make that safe, and a shared library
defeats them in a specific way:

| before                              | after                                     |
|-------------------------------------+-------------------------------------------|
| a change touched one app            | a =shared/= change touches *every* app    |
| blast radius was in the manifest    | the manifest still says =app:core=        |
| =change:standard= was defensible    | it is a coordinated change wearing a standard label |

*=apps/**= is no longer the deployable entity, and the classifier still thinks
it is.* The =apps/**=-blocks rule was a coarse but honest approximation while
the apps were self-contained; a shared library breaks the assumption it rests
on. =shared/= is classified =artifact= now, so it blocks — but it blocks
*equally*, with no signal that its blast radius is five times a single-app
change.

*It was nearly worse.* Before this refactor the classifier knew only =apps/=
and =router/=, so a change to =shared/= would have classified *=inert=* — the
highest-blast-radius change in the repo waved through as "nothing that can
appear in a deployed artifact". Verified, not theorised.

*Why do it anyway.* This repo exists to work out change control, not to be
convenient to deploy. A model that only stays coherent while the apps are
artificially independent has not been tested against the thing that actually
breaks monorepos. *We can be wrong in the near term to be more correct later* —
and the near-term wrongness is now visible and named rather than absent.

*What it means for the options.* It sharpens A2 specifically. Splitting the
deployable surface out of the monorepo does *not* remove this coupling: five
repos each importing an unversioned OneUI from a sixth deploy together exactly
as they do now. The work that pays is *versioning the shared artifact so a
consumer can lag* — and that is a different task from moving files, which is
the failure mode most likely to make A2 disappointing. See
=docs/cross-cutting-coupling.org= and issue #10.

*Open, not scheduled:* the classifier has no notion of blast radius, and
=idp.py change risk= — which does — is not wired into the pipeline. PR #5
carries five =app:*= labels and is labelled =change:standard= today.

*** Consequence — the environment tiers (was ADR 0002)

If promotion is automatic, then *which environments can be promoted into* is a
safety property, not a naming convention. Hence three tiers, with the port
number carrying the meaning:

| tier        | ports     | promotes? | watched?             |
|-------------+-----------+-----------+----------------------|
| *dev*       | 9000–9099 | no        | no — disposable      |
| *team*      | 9100–9199 | *no*      | listed, not monitored |
| *protected* | 9200–9299 | *yes*     | yes — this is the estate |

A tier is a property of the *address* rather than a rule someone remembers:
anything at 9100–9199 is a dead end by construction, and =ports.sh= refuses a
dev block that would cross into it. The team tier is *specified and not
provisioned* — its purpose is to make "cannot promote" structural, and the port
range plus the IDP's refusal to lease =staging= already achieve that. Trigger
to build it: the first team environment that outlives a worktree.

*** Consequence — what is worth watching

=./dashboard= shows the queue first, then protected, then team, then the open
changes. Dev is absent on purpose, and so are DORA metrics: every DORA metric
takes a deployment that *reached production* as its unit, so a forfeited window
and a revalidation are invisible to it — and under automatic promotion those
are the dominant cost. A row reading =Deploy Freq: 0/day= beside a pipeline
burning 10–20 validations per shipped change is worse than no row.

Evaluating MonoWatch is what made this concrete: 17 services × 3 environments,
every cell the same build, because a monorepo ships =apps/**= as one unit. *It
is a dashboard for the architecture options A and A2 would move us to.* If we
take one, this consequence expires and something MonoWatch-shaped becomes
correct.

** Option A2 — split the deployment out of the monorepo

Distinct from A, and cheaper. A says give each app a contract and its own path
to production; *A2 says only move the deployable surface out*, leaving the code
where it is. One repo per deployable, or a deployment repo that consumes built
artifacts, so a change to =pdp= cannot invalidate a change to =plp= because
they no longer share a trunk.

That is the whole of the contention: §The finding that decides this ADR
measured 10–20 validation runs per shipped change, and *every one of them is
caused by changes sharing a trunk they do not share a surface with*. Splitting
the trunk removes the invalidation without requiring the full contract work.

Cost: artifact promotion between repos, and a version-pinning problem where
there was none. Cheaper than A, and it does not fix cross-app journeys — those
still need somewhere to run, which is what staging remains for.

** Option A3 — release trains

Batch whatever is ready on a cadence and validate the batch once.
=docs/release-strategies.org= measured it: *0.58 validations per shipped change
against 13.63*, a 23× reduction, at 2.6 h mean latency against 12.5 h.

It attacks the ratio directly rather than the capacity, which makes it the only
option here that improves the number Option C cannot touch. Two findings that
came with it and should not be lost:

- the *throughput-optimal* train is red more often than green (P(green)
  0.41–0.53) — you batch until the batch usually fails, and staff for
  diagnosis;
- the analytic optimum sits at a *92–99% predicted emergency share* on this
  repo's own balance curve, i.e. unreachable. A short (≈1 h) train captures
  ~98% of the amortisation and stays on the reachable part.

*This is what the volume argument forces.* At Yegge's ~175 commits/day against
a 30-minute build, our own =capacity_report= gives 48 slots/day/berth — 3.6×
oversubscribed before a single gate fails. A train is not an optimisation
there; it is the only shape that fits.

** Option A4 — deploy main, not branches

Every deployment is a deployment *of main*; features are independently tested,
merged, and then main is what ships. Guard 0 becomes *unstateable* — there is
no branch to be behind — and the berth stops needing to bind a branch.

What it costs is what =docs/release-strategies.org= names: *"on main" becomes
intent, not evidence.* The merge happens before the production evidence exists,
so the thing that says a change is good is a merge rather than an observation.
It also leaves guard 4b's predicate merely relocated from a branch to a SHA,
not removed.

Pairs naturally with a *merge queue*, which builds each candidate against
current main and is guard 0 plus the merge-time half of guard 4b done natively
(§A merge queue is most of this model, natively). Unavailable on this plan; on
an enterprise account it is a switch.

** Option B — reservation + activation split (this decision)

Accepts the shared surface and manages the contention honestly: plans capacity,
gives guard 4b a home, makes forfeiture visible, and puts a number on the
ceiling. Does not raise the ceiling — see the table.

Cost: a scheduler, a reservation store, and forfeiture semantics people must
understand.

** Option C — keep immediate deployment, add berths

Berths *do* raise throughput once forfeited changes re-book (3/20 → 20/20 from
one berth to eight), so the earlier flat rejection was wrong. What they do not
do is reduce validation work per shipped change, which stays at 10–20×. And
adding berths silently reinstates D4 unless the merge is separately guarded —
Hypothesis found that at =berths=2=, and it is now fixed in =release.sh=.
*Accepted as a partial measure*: reach for it when latency is the complaint,
but it buys capacity at an unchanged unit cost, and it is not a substitute for
A or for a train.

** Option D — shorten the calendar quantum

Below 30 minutes the grid stops matching how humans schedule changes, and the
binding constraint is revalidation, not slot width. *Rejected as a primary
lever*; worth revisiting only after the surface is split.

** Option E — do nothing

Leaves the change schedule as a record rather than a control, and leaves guard
4b evaluated at request time where =experiments/001= showed it fails.
*Rejected.*

* Decision rule

In order of preference, and all of them beat B on the number that matters:

1. *A* — contracts plus a path to production per team. Removes the contention.
2. *A2* — split only the deployable surface out of the monorepo. Removes the
   shared trunk, which is what actually causes the invalidation, without the
   full contract work.
3. *A4 + merge queue* — deploy main, let the forge build each candidate against
   current main. Most of the front half of this model, natively, on enterprise.
4. *A3* — release trains. The only option that improves *validations per ship*
   (23×) rather than capacity. Mandatory at high commit volume.
5. *B* — this decision. Correct only when none of the above is reachable.

*B is the fallback, not the recommendation.* Choose it when the surface cannot
be split in the horizon you care about, and treat its measured ceiling as the
standing argument for eventually doing 1–4.

Do not choose B expecting throughput. B buys *predictability, legibility and a
correct place for the guards*. It does not buy capacity, and this ADR would
rather say so than be quoted later as a promise it never made.
